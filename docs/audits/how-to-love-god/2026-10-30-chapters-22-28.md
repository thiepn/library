### HLG-SRC-026 — Premodern migration research represented as contemporary-mission evidence

- **Severity:** Major (research source period / evidence-scope mismatch; no demonstrated false numerical statistic).
- **Exact source:** `src/content/works/how-to-love-god/chapters/26-cross-cultural-mission-and-the-global-church.md:76` and `chapters/l-sources-notes-and-further-reading.md:108` at parent HEAD, note 26.2.
- **Original chapter:** Migration has become a major missionary reality.<sup class="endnote-call" id="ref-26-2"><a href="#note-26-2">26.2</a></sup> Students, workers, refugees, businesspeople, pastors, and families carry faith across borders. Diaspora churches can become both receiving and sending communities.
- **Original note:** **26.2.** Hanciles documents migration as a major force in the making of global Christianity and emphasizes Christianity's increasingly multidirectional movement. Escobar likewise argues for mission “from everywhere to everyone,” challenging a simple West-to-rest model. See Hanciles, *Migration and the Making of Global Christianity*; Escobar, *The New Global Mission*.
- **Problem:** The note associates Hanciles's historical study with increasingly multidirectional **contemporary** movement, whereas the book specifically covers Christian expansion **through 1500 CE**. Escobar's 2003 book does address a shift to global multidirectional mission but is not contemporary demographic evidence. The original text could mislead the reader about source chronology.
- **Primary evidence:** Eerdmans publisher, Jehu J. Hanciles, *Migration and the Making of Global Christianity* (2021), explicit chronological scope [publisher](https://www.eerdmans.com/9780802875624/migration-and-the-making-of-global-christianity/); InterVarsity Press publisher, Samuel Escobar, *The New Global Mission* (2003), [publisher](https://www.ivpress.com/the-new-global-mission); Lausanne Movement contemporary network discussion [Diasporas](https://lausanne.org/network/diasporas).
- **Revised chapter:** Migration has shaped Christian witness across centuries and remains a major mission context today.<sup class="endnote-call" id="ref-26-2"><a href="#note-26-2">26.2</a></sup> Students, workers, refugees, businesspeople, pastors, and families carry faith across borders. Diaspora churches can become both receiving and sending communities.
- **Revised source note:** **26.2.** Hanciles documents migration's historical influence on Christian expansion from its beginnings through approximately 1500 CE; that study is not a measurement of contemporary migration or mission. Escobar's *The New Global Mission* (2003) argues for mission “from everywhere to everyone,” but its publication date should not be confused with current demographic data. For a contemporary qualitative mission-oriented discussion of diaspora communities, see the Lausanne Movement's “Diasporas” network. None of these sources establishes a present-day migration count or quantitative ministry effect. See Hanciles, *Migration and the Making of Global Christianity*; Escobar, *The New Global Mission*; Lausanne Movement, “Diasporas.”
- **Publication approach:** Historical and contemporary sources kept distinct, no numerical claim invented; Lausanne dated in evidence bibliography at audit date.

### HLG-CONF-027 — Unclear scope of Wesleyan-Holiness entire sanctification

- **Severity:** Minor (confessional representation and doctrinal specificity).
- **Exact source:** `src/content/works/how-to-love-god/chapters/27-persevering-in-love-through-a-lifetime.md:60`; `chapters/l-sources-notes-and-further-reading.md:114` note 27.2.
- **Original chapter:** Wesleyan-Holiness traditions use the language of Christian perfection or perfect love for a present-life work of grace characterized by wholehearted love and freedom from the reigning power of willful sin, while carefully distinguishing this from absolute sinlessness or omniscience in many formulations. Other Protestant traditions reject or reinterpret that doctrine and emphasize progressive sanctification continuing until glorification.
- **Original note:** **27.2.** Wesleyan-Holiness traditions use “Christian perfection,” “entire sanctification,” and “perfect love” for a present-life doctrine that, in careful formulations, is not identical to omniscience, freedom from every infirmity, or eschatological impeccability. See Church of the Nazarene, “Articles of Faith”; John Wesley, *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection*. Other Protestant traditions reject or reinterpret this doctrine, while all agree that final perfected holiness belongs to glorification.
- **Problem:** A characterization limited to wholehearted love and avoiding willful sin omits a major actual doctrinal claim of the explicitly cited Church of the Nazarene (entire sanctification *subsequent to regeneration* and cleansing from *original sin / depravity*) and the church's explicit distinction of the pure heart from mature character. This is substantive for fair confessional representation, without endorsing the doctrine as universal.
- **Primary evidence:** Church of the Nazarene, [official Articles of Faith / Article X](https://nazarene.org/what-we-believe/). For a contrast, [Orthodox Presbyterian Church, Westminster Confession XVII–XVIII](https://opc.org/wcf.html) teaches divine preservation/perseverance.
- **Revised chapter:** Wesleyan-Holiness traditions use the language of Christian perfection or perfect love for present-life sanctification, but their formulations should not be reduced to ordinary moral improvement. For example, the Church of the Nazarene explicitly teaches entire sanctification as a grace-given work subsequent to regeneration, cleansing from original sin and directing the heart toward wholehearted love; it also distinguishes this from mature character, which continues to grow. This is a particular confessional claim, not an uncontested description of every Christian's present experience. Other Protestant traditions reject or reinterpret this doctrine and emphasize progressive sanctification continuing until glorification.
- **Revised note:** **27.2.** The Church of the Nazarene's Article X describes entire sanctification as a grace-given work subsequent to regeneration, addressing original sin or depravity, and distinguishes a pure heart from mature character, which continues to grow. This is more specific than a generic description of moral improvement. Within Wesleyan-Holiness theology, “Christian perfection” or “perfect love” does not, in careful formulations, mean omniscience, freedom from every infirmity, or eschatological impeccability. See Church of the Nazarene, “Articles of Faith”; John Wesley, *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection*. Other Protestant traditions reject or reinterpret this doctrine, while all agree that final perfected holiness belongs to glorification.
- **Publication approach:** Clarify what that specific tradition asserts; preserve explicit Protestant disagreement and eschatological distinction.

## Sources checked and evidence boundaries

- [GRACE Independent Investigations](https://www.netgrace.org/independent-investigations) makes clear that independent church-related abuse investigations do **not** replace police criminal investigations. This corroborates Chapters 22/24's separation of authority and protection but is not an international legal standard.
- [Lausanne Cape Town Commitment](https://lausanne.org/statement/ctcommitment) rejects coercive evangelism, states an integrated commitment to mission and social responsibility, and supports Chapters 23–25's ethical separation of aid from conversion leverage.
- [OPC Westminster Confession XVII](https://opc.org/wcf.html) confirms Reformed perseverance; [Church of England, Thirty-Nine Articles XVI](https://www.churchofengland.org/prayer-and-worship/worship-texts-and-resources/book-common-prayer/articles-religion) treats sin/repentance after baptism; [Church of the Nazarene](https://nazarene.org/what-we-believe/) documents its own distinct entire-sanctification position. They cannot be flattened into one uniform Protestant view.
- [Eerdmans Hanciles book page](https://www.eerdmans.com/9780802875624/migration-and-the-making-of-global-christianity/) establishes pre-1500 historical scope; [IVP Escobar book page](https://www.ivpress.com/the-new-global-mission) establishes 2003 publication; [Lausanne Diasporas network](https://lausanne.org/network/diasporas) provides contemporary qualitative discussion. No current migration counts or survey outcomes were invented.
- Appendix L references to Sanneh, Twiss, GRACE, Diane Langberg and other monographs were inspected at the manuscript/citation level, but **full book contents were not obtained and independently analyzed**. Those claims remain eligible for full-source verification during publication audit.
