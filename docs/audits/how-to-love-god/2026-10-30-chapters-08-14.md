## Findings requiring a correction

### HLG-EXE-011 — Context of Philippians 2:12–13

- **Severity:** Minor — incomplete canonical/communal context; original theological point remains valid.
- **Exact source:** `src/content/works/how-to-love-god/chapters/11-growing-without-earning.md:16`, “God works, therefore we act.”
- **Before:** Philippians 2 places human working alongside God's working. The passage has been summarized in Christian tradition in many memorable ways, but Paul's point should be allowed to stand without turning a later slogan into his wording: God's action does not make Christian obedience passive. It grounds and empowers it.
- **Evidence:** Philippians 2:1–11 introduces the appeal to humility, unity and Christ's obedience; 2:12–18 addresses the congregation (plural exhortations). Relevant exegetical source: [Bible.org, Philippians 2:12–18](https://new.bible.org/seriespage/8-exhortation-unity-final-word-concerning-obedience-philippians-212-18) and [Philippians 2 study](https://new.bible.org/seriespage/philippians-2-0).
- **Correction:** Philippians 2 places human working alongside God's working. Paul addresses the Philippian congregation after calling for shared humility and unity in Christ (Philippians 2:1–11); his words should not be isolated into a private self-improvement formula. The passage has been summarized in Christian tradition in many memorable ways, but Paul's point should be allowed to stand without turning a later slogan into his wording: God's action does not make Christian obedience passive. It grounds and empowers it.
- **Reason:** Prevent isolation of this corporate exhortation into personal performance or individualistic habit optimization while preserving divine-initiative/real-obedience synthesis.

### HLG-EXE-014 — Different Gospel introductions to the Lord's Prayer

- **Severity:** Minor — source nuance underexplained; original approval of both prayer forms was defensible.
- **Exact source:** `src/content/works/how-to-love-god/chapters/14-prayer-and-communion.md:24`, “The prayer Jesus teaches.”
- **Before:** Christians may pray the prayer verbatim or use its movements as a pattern. The New Testament does not require one method. Its enduring value is the order of concern: God's glory, God's reign, daily need, reconciled relationships, moral dependence, and protection.
- **Evidence:** [Matthew 6:9–13 and Luke 11:2–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+6%3A9-13%2CLuke+11%3A2-4&version=NABRE); [NET notes on Luke 11:2–4](https://classic.net.bible.org/passage.php?mode=print&passage=luk+11%3A2-4); [St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology: Lord's Prayer](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/TheLordsPrayer). These sources distinguish Matthew's pattern wording and Luke's invitation to say the prayer.
- **Correction:** Christians may pray the prayer verbatim or use its movements as a pattern. Matthew introduces it with “pray like this” (Matthew 6:9), while Luke records Jesus saying “when you pray, say” (Luke 11:2). Neither direct recitation nor prayer shaped by its petitions should be treated as a technique that guarantees an outcome. Its enduring value includes the order of concern: God's glory, God's reign, daily need, reconciled relationships, moral dependence, and protection.
- **Reason:** Avoid appearing to diminish Luke's verbal instruction while neither mandating only verbatim recitation nor inventing a guarantee about outcomes.
