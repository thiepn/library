A person can be sincere and still be wrong about the object of devotion. That is an uncomfortable fact in a culture that often treats sincerity as the highest religious virtue. Christian love cannot be defined by intensity alone because the God Christians are called to love is not an idea produced by our desires. He is the God who speaks, acts, creates, judges, saves, and makes himself known.

## The question before the command

Deuteronomy's command to love God is inseparable from the confession of who God is (Deuteronomy 6:4–5). Israel is not invited to imagine the deity most attractive to them and then offer wholehearted devotion. The command comes within a covenant story in which the Lord has revealed himself, delivered a people, and claimed their allegiance. Before the question is *How intensely do I love?* the question is *Whom do I love?*

That order matters today. We can attach the word *God* to very different pictures: a permissive force who never judges; a distant ruler who must be appeased; a therapeutic helper whose main purpose is to stabilize our emotions; a guarantor of success; an abstract principle of love; or a projection of our preferred moral instincts. Some of these pictures preserve fragments of truth. None can be allowed to replace God's self-revelation.

Psalm 145, for example, praises God not as a blank object of spirituality but as King, Creator, provider, righteous judge, merciful helper, and faithful covenant Lord. Exodus 34 places mercy, patience, covenant faithfulness, forgiveness, and justice together. Scripture repeatedly refuses to let us preserve the divine qualities we find comforting while discarding those that unsettle us.

This is one reason theology matters to love. Accurate doctrine is not the same thing as love, but love cannot remain healthy while its object is steadily distorted. If I claim to love a person while refusing everything true about that person that contradicts my preferences, I increasingly love an invention. Something similar can happen in religion. The Christian does not earn God's favor by getting every doctrine right, but truth is relationally necessary.

## God makes himself known

The Bible's God is not hidden because creatures have discovered the right philosophical method. He reveals himself. Creation bears witness to his power and glory, but Scripture gives the redemptive story and covenant speech by which God's identity and purposes become known. In the Old Testament, God's name, acts, law, promises, judgments, and mercy disclose his character. In the New Testament, this revelation reaches its decisive center in Jesus Christ.

John's Gospel begins by locating the Son within the identity and life of God and then describes the Word becoming flesh (John 1:1–18). Jesus does more than bring information about God. The Son makes the Father known. Colossians speaks of Christ as the image of the invisible God and places creation, reconciliation, and the church around him (Colossians 1:15–20). Jesus can tell his disciples that seeing him is bound up with knowing the Father, while still speaking as the Son who relates to the Father (John 14:8–11).

This protects two truths. First, Christian God-love is irreducibly Christ-centered. A spirituality that uses Christian language while consistently resisting the person, teaching, cross, resurrection, and lordship of Jesus does not fit apostolic Christianity. Second, Christ-centeredness must not collapse the Father and the Son into one undifferentiated person. The New Testament gives us a relational, Trinitarian grammar: the Father sends and loves the Son; the Son reveals the Father and accomplishes redemption; the Spirit glorifies the Son, applies the work of Christ, and brings believers into filial communion.

## Father, Son, and Spirit

Christians worship one God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. That confession should shape a book about loving God from beginning to end rather than appearing as an introductory doctrinal box.

The gospel proceeds from the Father's love and purpose. The Son is the incarnate Lord who gives himself for sinners and brings them to God, not merely a moral example. The Spirit is the divine person who indwells believers, bears witness to their adoption, produces holiness, helps them pray, and unites the church in Christ—not religious atmosphere or spiritual energy.

Yet Scripture does not give a neat set of identical commands requiring Christians to express love toward each divine Person in exactly the same verbal form. We should not manufacture symmetry where the biblical language is more textured. The safer and richer claim is that Christian life is Trinitarian in source, mediation, communion, and goal. We come to the Father through the Son in the Spirit. We love the Son and keep his commands. We walk by the Spirit and set our minds on what belongs to the Spirit. We worship the one God who has made himself known as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

## Worthy above every rival

God's worth cannot be reduced to the benefits he gives. God gives life, food, friendship, healing, vocation, beauty, family, church, and countless ordinary goods. Gratitude for gifts is fitting. Yet Scripture repeatedly calls people beyond the gifts to the Giver.

Psalm 73 reaches a point where the psalmist can speak of God as his portion even amid bodily and emotional weakness. Jesus warns that treasure and the heart belong together and that a person cannot serve both God and wealth as masters (Matthew 6:19–24). Creation is not the problem. Created goods are good precisely because they are creatures and gifts, not gods.

This exposes one of the deepest confusions in religious life. We may want God mainly as the means to something else: health, marriage, children, safety, emotional peace, a successful ministry, financial security, social respectability, national prosperity, or protection from suffering. Many of these are legitimate things to ask for. The danger appears when our allegiance depends on receiving them. If God must continually deliver the outcome I demand in order to remain worthy, then the outcome has begun to sit in judgment over God.

Mature Christians still grieve the loss of created goods and experience anger, confusion, or disappointment; the Psalms are far too honest to deny that. Supreme love means that no created good occupies God's place as final Lord, final hope, or final measure of worth.

## Knowledge that becomes worship

Knowing truth about God is necessary, but knowledge can be possessed in a way that does not become love. Paul can warn that knowledge may inflate pride while love builds up (1 Corinthians 8). His point is not that ignorance is spiritual. It is that knowledge detached from Godward humility and neighbor-love can become an instrument of self-exaltation.

A person may understand the doctrine of the Trinity and still be cruel. Another may defend divine holiness while hiding personal dishonesty. Someone may read theology for years while becoming less teachable, less patient, and more eager to correct than to worship. The answer is not less truth. It is truth received as truth about God.

Romans 12 moves from the mercies of God to embodied worship, renewed thinking, humility, gifts, love, patience, generosity, peace, and overcoming evil with good. Truth does not stay in abstraction. It reorders the person toward God and neighbor.

That gives us a simple starting test. When you learn something true about God, what happens next? Does truth become worship? Does holiness become more serious because God is holy? Does mercy become more precious because God is merciful? Does Christ become more central? Does knowledge make you humbler about the distance between Creator and creature? Does it help you love other people more truthfully?

These questions are not a salvation checklist. They describe the direction in which truthful knowledge is meant to move.

### Response

Take one assumption about God that you rarely examine—perhaps an assumption about his goodness, holiness, patience, sovereignty, fatherhood, or nearness. Place it beside a substantial biblical passage rather than a slogan. Ask what the passage actually reveals. Then turn one clear truth into worship. Do not begin by measuring the strength of your feelings. Begin with God himself. Christian love is not love for the deity we would prefer to exist, but a growing response to the God who has made himself known in Christ. The next question is what such love actually consists of.
