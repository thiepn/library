## Source method

Scripture is the final theological authority for the argument. Other source types answer different questions and should not be confused with one another.

**Biblical and exegetical sources** help establish what a text means in context and how it functions canonically.

**Historical sources** establish what people, churches, and mission movements actually did or said. A Bible verse cannot prove a historical event.

**Systematic and confessional sources** help map doctrinal synthesis and identify legitimate Protestant disagreements.

**Empirical and clinical sources** support claims about psychology, mental health, behavior, or current research. Biblical narratives should not be used as retrospective clinical diagnoses.

**Missiological and demographic sources** support current descriptions of mission practice, people-group tools, migration, and global Christianity. Numerical claims must be dated and tied to definitions and methods.

**Legal and safeguarding sources** are jurisdiction-sensitive. General biblical principles do not replace current specialist advice on reporting, child protection, employment law, or criminal procedure.

## Scripture quotation policy

Unless otherwise indicated, direct English Bible quotations in this edition use the Berean Standard Bible. Direct quotation remains selective. The BSB was dedicated to the public domain on April 30, 2023, and is identified in the copyright notice for reader transparency.

Where this manuscript paraphrases a passage, the reference still allows readers to examine the text themselves. Paraphrase is not presented inside quotation marks as though it were exact translation wording.

## Historical and theological voices

Named theologians should be represented from actual works rather than from remembered slogans. Exact quotations require edition and location verification. Paraphrase is preferable when exact wording is unnecessary.

The source base draws from historic Western theologians and from contemporary and global Christian scholarship. Global voices should be included because their work materially improves the argument, not to satisfy a representation quota. No writer should be treated as spokesperson for a continent.

## Psychology and clinical material

Clinical terms are used sparingly and descriptively. Depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, scrupulosity, anhedonia, trauma-related distress, and medication effects are not defined by biblical proof texts. These chapters offer pastoral distinctions, not diagnosis or treatment protocols.

Where serious impairment or safety risk is present, the manuscript directs readers toward competent local care rather than prescribing medication changes or guaranteed recovery pathways.

## Mission history and current mission claims

Mission history should be narrated with both gratitude and moral seriousness. Translation, education, medical service, church planting, sacrifice, and local Christian agency should not be erased; neither should empire, racism, paternalism, coercion, or cultural suppression where historical evidence establishes them.

Modern mission categories such as “unreached people group” are strategic tools. They should never be presented as if they were identical to biblical nations language. Current statistics should be rechecked near publication and may be omitted when the argument does not require a number.

## Further reading

Readers are encouraged to compare interpretations, consult local pastors and teachers, and read beyond the sources listed here. Appendix K offers a selective reader-facing list; complete publication details appear in the Recommended Reading Bibliography below. The purpose of a source apparatus is not to make the manuscript look academic. It is to make substantive claims traceable and to keep different kinds of evidence in their proper place.

## Chapter-Grouped Source Notes

These notes document the main research-dependent claims prioritized in the final whole-book audit. They support empirical, historical, missiological, and safeguarding claims without replacing Scripture as the theological authority of the argument.

### Chapter 7 — Assurance, Scrupulosity, and the Reassurance Loop

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-7-1"></span>**7.1.** Scrupulosity is commonly discussed within obsessive-compulsive phenomena involving religious or moral obsessions and compulsive neutralization/checking. The treatment literature is smaller and more heterogeneous than the general OCD literature, so this chapter uses it only to justify recognition, caution about repetitive reassurance, and referral—not diagnosis or treatment instructions. See Toprak and Özçelik, “Psychotherapies for the Treatment of Scrupulosity”; Miller and Hedges, “Scrupulosity Disorder.” [↩](#ref-7-1)

### Chapter 10 — Love Desires: Affection, Delight, Joy, and Holy Longing

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-10-1"></span>**10.1.** Contemporary reward research distinguishes incentive “wanting” from hedonic “liking” and from reflective valuation. These constructs are not a Christian doctrine of desire, but they support the narrower claim that a strong momentary urge need not equal a person's deepest endorsed allegiance. See Berridge, “Separating Desire from Prediction of Outcome Value”; Berridge and Robinson, “Liking, Wanting and the Incentive-Sensitization Theory of Addiction.” [↩](#ref-10-1)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-10-2"></span>**10.2.** Modern emotion science contains multiple theoretical frameworks and treats emotion as more complex than conscious feeling alone. Clinical and emotion-regulation research also cautions against inferring character or spiritual condition directly from one affective state. See Barrett et al., “The Experience of Emotion”; Sheppes, Suri, and Gross, “Emotion Regulation and Psychopathology”; Lincoln, Schulze, and Renneberg, “The Role of Emotion Regulation.” [↩](#ref-10-2)

### Chapter 11 — Growing Without Earning

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-11-1"></span>**11.1.** Habit research supports the modest behavioral claim that repetition in recurring contexts can increase automaticity and reduce initiation friction, while formation rates vary widely by person, behavior, and context. It does not establish that repeated spiritual behavior automatically produces holiness or love. See Wood and Rünger, “Psychology of Habit”; Lally et al., “How Are Habits Formed?”; “Time to Form a Habit.” [↩](#ref-11-1)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-11-2"></span>**11.2.** Attention is shaped by current goals, physical salience, and learned selection/reward history. That evidence supports caution about environments that repeatedly capture attention; it does not establish a deterministic chain from attention to spiritual affection. See Anderson et al., “The Past, Present, and Future of Selection History.” [↩](#ref-11-2)

### Chapter 17 — When God Feels Distant

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-17-1"></span>**17.1.** Empirical studies of “spiritual dryness” describe a phenomenological/religious-struggle construct and report multiple associated experiences and coping patterns. The cited studies are population-specific and largely observational; they do not establish one universal cause or automatic spiritual benefit. See Büssing, “Strategies to Cope with Phases of Spiritual Dryness”; Büssing et al., “Experience of Spiritual Dryness and Acedia Symptoms.” [↩](#ref-17-1)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-17-2"></span>**17.2.** Experimental sleep research gives strong evidence that sleep loss can alter positive affect, anxiety, arousal, mood, and emotion regulation. The manuscript therefore treats bodily depletion as one possible contributor without diagnosing a spiritual state from sleep alone. See Palmer et al., “Sleep Loss and Emotion.” [↩](#ref-17-2)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-17-3"></span>**17.3.** Depression can include broad loss of interest or pleasure, energy and concentration changes, and other clinically relevant symptoms. Anhedonia is heterogeneous rather than a single spiritual or psychological mechanism. See National Institute of Mental Health, “Depression”; Pizzagalli, “Toward a Better Understanding of the Mechanisms and Pathophysiology of Anhedonia.” [↩](#ref-17-3)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-17-4"></span>**17.4.** Scrupulosity literature supports distinguishing unwanted intrusive content from deliberate endorsement and cautions against compulsive neutralization/checking. The chapter deliberately stops at recognition and referral. See Toprak and Özçelik; Miller and Hedges. [↩](#ref-17-4)

### Chapter 19 — Rival Loves in a Distracted and Disordered World

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-19-1"></span>**19.1.** Augustine develops influential accounts of rightly and wrongly ordered love, enjoyment, use, and the relation of creaturely goods to God, especially in *On Christian Doctrine* Book I and throughout relevant portions of the *Confessions*. This manuscript uses “ordered love” as a historical-theological synthesis, not as Augustine's exact wording in every place and not as a substitute for the chapter's biblical argument. [↩](#ref-19-1)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-19-2"></span>**19.2.** For historical context on business models organized around capturing and reselling human attention, see Tim Wu, *The Attention Merchants*. The source supports the broad economic history; it is not used as technical proof about every platform, algorithm, or individual user. [↩](#ref-19-2)

### Chapter 20 — Spiritual Warfare Without Fear or Speculation

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-20-1"></span>**20.1.** Opoku Onyinah's Ghanaian Pentecostal study of witchcraft and demonology provides an important internal Christian/African contextual witness to the interaction of indigenous concepts, Pentecostal exorcism, fear, and pastoral practice. It should not be generalized to all African societies or all Pentecostal churches. See Onyinah, *Pentecostal Exorcism*. [↩](#ref-20-1)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-20-2"></span>**20.2.** The chapter's theological prohibition of coercion rests on Scripture and Christian ethics. For practical abuse-prevention and safeguarding frameworks relevant to church settings, see GRACE's abuse-prevention, policy, training, and safeguarding resources. GRACE is a U.S.-based organization; local law and denominational procedures remain controlling for jurisdiction-specific duties. [↩](#ref-20-2)

### Chapter 24 — Church Authority, Harm, Safeguarding, and Restoration

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-24-1"></span>**24.1.** Oakley, Kinmond, and Blundell examine spiritual abuse and implications for counselling/psychotherapy, including the difficulty of naming harm where religious authority, identity, and relationships are involved. The source supports recognition and care considerations; it does not define the book's doctrine of church authority. See “Responding Well to Spiritual Abuse.” [↩](#ref-24-1)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-24-2"></span>**24.2.** GRACE's current safeguarding and abuse-response materials emphasize prevention, written policy, training, reporting pathways, survivor-sensitive practice, and the potential need for independent investigation. These resources are practical rather than universally binding ecclesiology or law. Churches must follow applicable local law and competent professional guidance. [↩](#ref-24-2)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-24-3"></span>**24.3.** Diane Langberg's *Redeeming Power* is a pastoral-psychological Christian treatment of power, vulnerability, abuse, institutional self-protection, and the responsibilities of Christian leaders. It is used here as a dialogue resource, not as an exhaustive polity standard. [↩](#ref-24-3)

### Chapter 26 — Cross-Cultural Mission and the Global Church

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-26-1"></span>**26.1.** Sanneh's work on Christian translation highlights vernacularization and the capacity of Christian faith to take local linguistic and cultural form. Twiss offers a Native American Christian critique of colonial/cowboy forms of mission and argues for indigenous Christian expression. These works do not imply that all pre-Christian cultural practices are compatible with Christianity; they support the narrower rejection of unnecessary cultural assimilation. See Sanneh, *Translating the Message*; Twiss, *Rescuing the Gospel from the Cowboys*. [↩](#ref-26-1)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-26-2"></span>**26.2.** Hanciles documents migration as a major force in the making of global Christianity and emphasizes Christianity's increasingly multidirectional movement. Escobar likewise argues for mission “from everywhere to everyone,” challenging a simple West-to-rest model. See Hanciles, *Migration and the Making of Global Christianity*; Escobar, *The New Global Mission*. [↩](#ref-26-2)

### Chapter 27 — Persevering in Love Through a Lifetime

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-27-1"></span>**27.1.** The chapter's summary is intentionally comparative. Reformed confessions classically teach preservation/perseverance of the truly regenerate; Wesleyan-Arminian and Free Will Baptist traditions allow final apostasy; Lutheran and Anglican formulations require their own nuance and should not be collapsed into either side. Representative primary sources include Westminster Confession XVII–XVIII; the Lutheran *Book of Concord*; the United Methodist Articles of Religion; the Church of the Nazarene Articles of Faith; the Anglican Articles of Religion; the Baptist Faith and Message; and relevant Free Will Baptist confessional material. The note documents diversity rather than adjudicating the dispute. [↩](#ref-27-1)

<span class="endnote-entry" id="note-27-2"></span>**27.2.** Wesleyan-Holiness traditions use “Christian perfection,” “entire sanctification,” and “perfect love” for a present-life doctrine that, in careful formulations, is not identical to omniscience, freedom from every infirmity, or eschatological impeccability. See Church of the Nazarene, “Articles of Faith”; John Wesley, *A Plain Account of Christian Perfection*. Other Protestant traditions reject or reinterpret this doctrine, while all agree that final perfected holiness belongs to glorification. [↩](#ref-27-2)
