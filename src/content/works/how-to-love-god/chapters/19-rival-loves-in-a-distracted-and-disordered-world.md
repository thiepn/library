A created good becomes dangerous when it is asked to be God. That sentence is easy to repeat and hard to apply. Christians can call everything an idol until the word loses precision, or they can reserve idolatry for statues and miss modern forms of rival allegiance.

Rival loves are competing trusts, valuations, loyalties, and patterns that displace God's supremacy. They must be discerned without treating every strong desire, pleasure, or habit as idolatry.

## Idolatry and disordered love

Scripture's explicit language is worship, exchange, covetousness, mastery, loyalty, and false gods. Christian thinkers such as Augustine have helpfully described sin in terms of wrongly ordered loves. That phrase is a theological synthesis, not a biblical quotation.<sup class="endnote-call" id="ref-19-1"><a href="#note-19-1">19.1</a></sup>

Its value is that sin often does not consist in loving an evil object. It consists in loving a good thing as ultimate, or loving it in a way that violates God's order. Family, nation, work, pleasure, security, ministry, reputation, and knowledge can all be genuine goods. They become rivals when they claim the allegiance, trust, or moral permission that belongs to God.

The test is not “Do I enjoy this?” but “What place does it occupy?”

## Individual rivals

**Money** can become more than a tool or provision. It promises control, status, insulation from dependence, and the ability to shape outcomes.

**Sex and romance** can become sources of identity and salvation, or areas where desire is permitted to overrule God's will.

**Success and productivity** can become a verdict: if I achieve, I matter; if I fail, I disappear.

**Approval** can make human opinion more determinative than obedience.

**Comfort** can turn suffering or sacrifice into automatic evidence that something is wrong.

**Control** can imitate trust while refusing creaturely limits.

These diagnoses are useful only when concrete. Generic vice lists can make everyone feel vaguely guilty while changing nothing.

## Collective rivals

Idolatry is also corporate. Nations, ethnic identities, political movements, family honor, prosperity, institutional reputation, and group survival can become sacred loyalties.

Cultural belonging is not therefore suspect. Scripture anticipates redeemed peoples and nations, and ordinary loyalty to family and community can be good. The problem occurs when group identity becomes morally unquestionable, when outsiders are dehumanized, or when the church protects its reputation at the cost of truth.

Collective idols can be harder to see because they are socially rewarded. What feels like faithfulness may actually be conformity to the expectations of one's group.

## Digital attention as one modern domain

Digital systems can intensify distraction, comparison, pornography, outrage, consumerism, and constant stimulation. Algorithms often compete for attention because attention has economic value.<sup class="endnote-call" id="ref-19-2"><a href="#note-19-2">19.2</a></sup> These realities deserve practical thought.

But digital life is not the universal defining problem of modern Christianity. Billions of people experience technology differently, with varied access, costs, languages, infrastructure, and social effects. Christian counsel should not assume a high-income, always-connected Western user as the human default.

Where digital patterns do interfere with love, simple measures can help: remove an app, change notification settings, avoid a device in a vulnerable context, establish screen-free times, use accountability, or replace passive consumption with embodied presence. These are prudential choices, not divine commands.

No pop-neuroscience claim about “dopamine” is needed to justify wisdom about attention.

## Wanting is not always choosing

Desire must also be interpreted carefully. Galatians 5 describes conflict within Christian life. A believer can experience impulses opposed to the Spirit while resisting them.

An urge is morally relevant, but it is not identical to deliberate allegiance. Intrusive thoughts, conditioned responses, bodily appetite, trauma-related reactions, and habits can operate without revealing a person's final values in a simple way.

Yet complexity must not become an excuse. Repeatedly choosing and nurturing a pattern matters. The distinction helps us repent accurately: not claiming guilt for every unwanted mental event, and not hiding chosen sin behind psychological language.

## Repentance means reordering allegiance under grace

The answer to rival love goes beyond removal. If approval rules us, deleting social media without learning to live before God may simply move the idol elsewhere. If money rules us, a temporary giving challenge can expose fear but cannot by itself create trust. If ministry success rules us, a break may reveal the problem but will not heal identity.

Repentance returns to God under grace. It names the rival, receives Christ again as sufficient, obeys concretely, and builds practices that support the new allegiance. Sometimes this includes giving, fasting, confession, limits, service, or changed routines. None is a magical replacement mechanism.

There is another dimension as well: some opposition to love involves spiritual powers, not only habit or flesh.

### Response

Choose one rival that is concrete enough to name. Ask what promise it makes: security, approval, pleasure, control, identity, innocence, belonging, or escape. Then ask what obedience under God would look like. Do not call every unwanted urge an idol, and do not call every cultural loyalty evil. Focus on mastery and allegiance. Make one practical change that serves repentance, but do not treat the change as the cure. The deeper movement is back toward God through grace.
