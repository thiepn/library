The question *How do I love God?* reaches beneath almost every other question in the Christian life. Prayer, holiness, worship, endurance, service, mission, and love for other people all depend on what we believe God is like and how we respond to him. Yet the question can become painful. A Christian may know that love for God is the greatest command and still feel spiritually cold. Another person may feel intense religious emotion while resisting ordinary obedience. Someone else may work hard in church while quietly trying to earn the acceptance that can only be received through Christ.

Scripture gives a larger picture. Love for God includes affection without being reducible to feeling, takes obedient form without collapsing into compliance, and depends on truth without becoming mere knowledge. Nor can it be produced by the right schedule, worship atmosphere, prayer method, or spiritual discipline. Christian love begins farther back than our effort. God acts first. He reveals himself, gives his Son, raises Christ from the dead, calls sinners to repentance and faith, gives his Spirit, receives believers in Christ, and brings them into communion with himself. Our love is real, active, and commanded, but it is responsive before it is self-originating.

The argument therefore begins with God rather than with technique. It asks who the God of Scripture is, what love toward him actually means, and why Jesus places love of neighbor beside love of God. It then turns to grace: sin, new life, acceptance in Christ, adoption, assurance, and communion with the triune God. Only after that foundation does it consider obedience, desire, practices, church, Scripture, prayer, worship, repentance, spiritual dryness, suffering, rival loves, spiritual warfare, relationships, vocation, church authority, witness, mission, perseverance, and new creation.

The order matters. If practices come before grace, spiritual formation easily becomes performance. If affection is detached from truth, intensity can be mistaken for maturity. If obedience is detached from grace, holiness becomes a wage offered to God. If private devotion is detached from church and neighbor, a person may appear deeply spiritual while remaining difficult to love. If suffering is treated as a simple diagnostic tool, wounded people can be burdened with explanations Scripture does not give. If mission is detached from humility and the global church, zeal can become paternalism or coercion. Each part of the Christian life needs the others.

Scripture is the final theological authority for the argument. Historical theologians, pastors, psychologists, clinicians, missiologists, and global Christian voices can clarify questions, expose assumptions, and supply evidence within their proper fields, but none of them governs the biblical text. Faithful Protestants disagree on several secondary questions, including the relation of regeneration and faith, the mode and subjects of baptism, the presence of Christ in the Lord's Supper, and the possibility of final apostasy. Those differences will remain visible rather than being hidden for smoother prose.

These chapters are written for Christian readers who want theological depth without having to read a technical systematic theology. Technical terms appear where they genuinely help, but plain English comes first. The purpose is to help readers know God truly, rest in Christ, walk by the Spirit, love people concretely, remain faithful under pressure, and hope for the day when love is made complete in resurrection and new creation.

Read slowly enough to look up the biblical passages. Use the chapter-end Responses as invitations, not scorecards. If a suggested practice does not fit your health, responsibilities, safety, resources, family, culture, or access to a local church, adapt it. No reading plan, prayer rhythm, journal, rule of life, or thirty-day exercise can manufacture love or place God in your debt.

Some readers will need to enter the book nonlinearly. If fear about salvation is consuming you, begin with Chapter 7 and Appendix H. If God feels distant or desire has collapsed, see Chapter 17 and Appendix D. If you are suffering or living with unanswered prayer, Chapter 18 is designed for that pressure. If you are dealing with serious harm, forgiveness, trust, or boundaries, see Chapter 22. If church authority has become coercive or unsafe, see Chapter 24. Questions of witness, missionary calling, and cross-cultural mission are treated in Chapters 25 and 26.

The central claim is simple even when its implications are wide: God is worthy of wholehearted love, and the love he commands is not the price by which sinners obtain him. In Christ, grace comes first. Christian love is the growing, whole-person response of those who are being drawn into the life for which they were made—communion with the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit, expressed in holiness and love of neighbor, and finally perfected in the new creation.
