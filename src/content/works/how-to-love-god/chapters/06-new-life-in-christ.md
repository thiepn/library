Christian conversion can be described with several biblical images: birth, resurrection, cleansing, turning, faith, union with Christ, receiving the Spirit, adoption, and entrance into a new people. These images belong together, but they should not be flattened into one formula or arranged as though every Protestant tradition agrees on one finely ordered sequence.

The simpler question is this: what does God give when he creates a new Godward life?

## The promise of a renewed heart

The Old Testament anticipates divine action upon the heart in several distinct ways. Deuteronomy 30 looks forward to God changing the heart so that his people love him. The text directly contributes divine initiative, inward transformation, and God-love. It should not be made to contain the entire later doctrine of regeneration by itself.

Jeremiah 31 promises a new covenant characterized by God's instruction written within, covenant knowledge, belonging, and forgiveness. That passage does not itself say “new birth,” nor does its central paragraph contain the same Spirit language as Ezekiel. Its contribution is covenant renewal, internalized instruction, knowledge of God, and forgiveness.

Ezekiel 36 speaks more directly of cleansing, a new heart, a new spirit, God's Spirit, and a resulting walk in God's ways. Together these passages create a strong canonical trajectory of divine heart-renewal. The later Christian term *regeneration* is a theological synthesis applied within the fuller biblical story; it should not be read back as though each Old Testament text used the same technical vocabulary.

## New birth and divine initiative

Jesus tells Nicodemus that entrance into God's kingdom requires birth from above or new birth and connects this work with the Spirit (John 3:1–8). The image emphasizes dependence. A person does not cause his own birth.

That does not make repentance and faith unreal. The gospel calls people to turn and believe. Acts portrays hearers responding to the message. The Epistles command faith and perseverance. Christian theology has long asked how divine initiative and human response should be ordered conceptually. Across these differences, the shared Protestant center is that saving response depends on grace, not autonomous human spiritual power.

New life is therefore not a self-improvement project. The gospel does more than help the old self become more religious. God acts to create life where sin has produced death and alienation.

## Repentance and faith

Repentance and faith belong together as a grace-enabled turning toward God. Faith receives Christ and rests on God's promise. Repentance turns from sin and false lordship. Neither is a work offered to purchase salvation.

Repentance is more than regret about consequences. It tells the truth about sin and changes direction. Faith is more than intellectual agreement. It entrusts oneself to Christ. Yet both can be weak and real. The strength of faith does not become a second savior.

This matters for love because the new Godward life is not built on a direct command to produce affection. It begins with receiving Christ, turning from rival lordship, and being brought into a new relation to God. Love grows within that life.

## Union with Christ and the Spirit

The New Testament repeatedly describes believers as being “in Christ” and Christ as dwelling in them through the Spirit. Union with Christ is not one more item on a salvation checklist. It names the profound reality that saving life is bound up with the crucified and risen Lord. Believers share in the benefits of his work because they belong to him.

Different traditions use the language of union and participation with varying emphases. We should not let one metaphor swallow justification, adoption, sanctification, or the future resurrection. Those benefits remain distinguishable. Yet Christian life cannot be explained adequately as God merely issuing a legal verdict from a distance. The Spirit indwells, gives life, produces fruit, helps prayer, leads believers in holiness, and assures them of filial belonging.

Romans 8 is especially important because it refuses to separate these themes. No condemnation, life in the Spirit, mortification of sin, adoption, suffering, inheritance, prayer, and future glory appear within one sweeping argument. The passage does not invite us to turn them into an artificial ladder. It shows the density of saving life in Christ.

## Adoption and a new identity

Adoption means that God receives believers as his children. It is related to justification but not identical to it. Justification addresses righteous standing before God; adoption brings the relational privilege of belonging to the Father's household.

This filial identity changes the emotional grammar of obedience. Christians are not slaves trying to keep a volatile master calm. They approach God as Father through the Son by the Spirit. That does not make reverence disappear. Children of God still worship the Holy One. It changes the relation between holiness and fear: obedience is no longer an attempt to escape condemnation by achieving enough merit.

Adoption will become important in the next two chapters because it supports assurance and communion. It also prepares suffering: children may experience fatherly formation without interpreting hardship as rejection.

## Already renewed, not yet perfected

New life is real without being complete. The New Testament can speak decisively about believers' new identity while also commanding ongoing mortification, renewal, growth, repentance, and perseverance.

This protects us from two errors. One is cynical minimalism: if Christians still struggle, perhaps nothing has actually changed. The other is perfectionism: if new life is real, perhaps serious struggle proves a person has never been renewed. Scripture gives a more patient account. The Spirit creates real fruit amid conflict. Believers can grieve the Spirit, fall into sin, repent, grow, decline, recover, suffer, and mature.

Whole-person love therefore develops within an already-and-not-yet life. The Christian is not waiting to become a child of God by loving well enough, nor pretending that present love is complete.

### Response

Do not reduce new life to a chronology you must master before you can trust Christ. Receive the center: God acts in grace, calls you to repent and believe, joins saving life to Christ, gives the Spirit, and brings believers into his family. If your attention has been fixed on whether your spiritual experience is dramatic enough, shift it toward the Lord who gives life. If there is sin to turn from, turn. If faith feels weak, bring weak faith to a strong Savior. The new Godward life begins in grace and grows toward love.
