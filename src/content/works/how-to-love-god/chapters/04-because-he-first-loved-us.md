The command to love God can awaken desire, but it can also expose failure. If the Christian life begins with an inspection of the strength of our love, we will either become proud or despairing. Scripture gives a different starting point: God's love is prior.

First John grounds Christian love in the fact that God loved first. The gospel is not the story of morally promising people gradually becoming lovable enough for God. It is God's gracious action toward sinners in and through his Son. That order becomes the grammar of everything that follows.

## Grace precedes response

Grace makes human response possible without making it unreal. People repent, believe, obey, worship, love, pray, serve, and persevere; none of these responses creates the saving initiative it answers.

This matters because the heart continually tries to turn gifts into wages. We may think God will be more willing to receive us if we pray better, feel more deeply, conquer a particular sin, read more consistently, serve more sacrificially, or become more useful. Those actions may matter greatly as obedience. None can become the price of divine acceptance.

The gospel moves in the other direction. God acts in Christ. The sinner receives. The believer responds. Sanctification then becomes real growth in holiness and love, but the sequence of grace and response is not reversed every time we fail.

## Love demonstrated in Christ

God's prior love is not a vague divine benevolence detached from the cross. The New Testament points to the giving of the Son and Christ's self-giving death as decisive displays of divine love. Romans 5 speaks of God's love being demonstrated toward sinners through Christ's death. First John links God's love with sending the Son. Second Corinthians describes Christ's love as a constraining reality that redirects life away from self-centered living (2 Corinthians 5:14–15).

The cross tells us something about both God's love and our condition. Love is costly and holy. Sin is not trivial. Reconciliation requires more than emotional reassurance. Christ gives himself to bring sinners to God.

We must also speak carefully about the cross. The Son truly suffers. Jesus' cry of abandonment belongs to the horror of his passion. Christian theology confesses the saving significance of his death and the judgment borne there. But we should not describe the cross as an ontological rupture inside the Trinity, as though the Father ceased to love the Son or the divine life broke apart. The relationship between the Father and the Son is not dissolved at Calvary. The mystery is not improved by language stronger than Scripture warrants.

## Receiving before proving

The grace-first structure changes how spiritual failure is handled. Suppose a believer sees real coldness, selfishness, dishonesty, or disobedience. The appropriate response is not to deny the sin. Grace does not make repentance unnecessary. But neither should repentance become an attempt to make oneself acceptable enough to return.

Christian repentance comes back through Christ. Confession tells the truth because forgiveness is not secured by concealment. Obedience follows because grace trains rather than excuses. Gratitude grows because God's action is prior.

This is why justification must later be distinguished from sanctification. Believers need a stable answer to the question, *On what basis am I accepted?* If that answer becomes “the current quality of my love,” then every fluctuation in affection and every failure in obedience threatens the foundation. Chapter 7 will develop the doctrine in detail. For now the essential order is enough: love is fruit, not the ground on which God justifies.

## The Spirit and the experience of God's love

Romans 5 speaks of God's love being poured into believers' hearts through the Holy Spirit. The safest reading in context is primarily God's love for us made experientially known, not a standalone statement that the Spirit pours our love for God into us. That reading strengthens the grace-first point. The Spirit does more than command greater affection; he brings the love of God home to the believer.

Elsewhere Scripture clearly connects the Spirit with new birth, adoption, prayer, holiness, fruit, and the mortification of sin. Christian love is therefore Spirit-enabled without requiring us to make one verse prove every aspect of that claim.

This protects against two mistakes. The first is self-generation: trying to create spiritual affection by force. The second is passivity: imagining that because the Spirit works, the believer does nothing. Scripture places divine agency and human response together. We will return to that relation in Chapter 11.

## Different relations of divine love

Christians sometimes speak as if every biblical use of God's love means exactly the same relational reality. Scripture is richer. God shows benevolent goodness toward creation. He sets covenant love upon his people. The Father loves the Son. God loves sinners in the giving of Christ. Jesus speaks of relational enjoyment associated with obedience. Hebrews speaks of fatherly discipline. These should not be collapsed into one flat category.

Disobedience does not make Christ's work insufficient or place the justified believer back under divine hatred until performance improves. Sin still damages lived fellowship, grieves the Spirit, harms people, and calls for repentance. Secure acceptance and serious relationship belong together.

More precise categories—justification, adoption, communion, discipline—will matter in their proper place, but grace remains the controlling grammar.

## What grace must rescue and renew

Grace does not create the need for salvation. Sin does. God's prior love reveals both the depth of the problem and the character of the rescue.

If our problem were merely lack of information, better teaching would be enough. If it were only weak habits, a stronger routine would solve it. If it were only emotional flatness, a more intense experience might repair us. Scripture describes something deeper: false worship, unbelief, rebellion, disordered allegiance, guilt, corruption, and death. We need more than instruction; we need new life.

Fallen people remain capable of natural affection, civic good, family love, appreciation of beauty, sacrifice, and religious desire. The narrower theological claim is that sinners do not autonomously generate the saving, supreme, rightly ordered love for the true God that the greatest command requires. Grace must do what self-improvement cannot.

Grace must therefore confront the condition from which our love needs rescue.

### Response

Begin today with God's prior action rather than the temperature of your response. Thank him specifically for Christ rather than thanking him vaguely for being helpful. If there is known sin, confess it without using shame as payment. If you have been trying to prove your seriousness through spiritual activity, name the bargain you have been offering God. Then obey the next clear command as a response to grace, not as a purchase. Christian love is active, but it is never the achievement that makes Christ willing to receive you. We love because grace has already moved toward us.
