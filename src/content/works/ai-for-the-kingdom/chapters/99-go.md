Much has changed. Christians can translate, research, learn, create, analyze, and build with capabilities that only recently required specialists, significant money, or large organizations. Some missionary barriers that were once stubbornly expensive can now be reduced.

We should reduce them. If artificial intelligence can help an underserved language gain useful digital tools, use it. If it can release missionaries from clerical work, use it. If it can make theological resources more accessible, improve translation workflows, or help a small ministry build something it could never otherwise afford, those are gifts worth stewarding.

But the mission has not been transferred. Christ has not commissioned a machine. He has commissioned his people. That is why greater capability does not make truth, judgment, responsibility, or community less important. It makes them more important. Christians still have to decide what should be trusted, what should be delegated, whose information should be protected, which relationships require human presence, and whether the technology is strengthening the people it is meant to serve.

Some ministries should use far more AI than they do today. Others should refuse particular uses. Faithfulness will not produce one universal level of adoption. Mission governs technology.

That principle should direct the Church toward places where artificial intelligence may matter most—not merely where it makes already capable Christians faster, but where it can reduce inequalities in access to language tools, theological education, specialized knowledge, accessibility, and technical capacity.

And that work should be done with the Christians whose languages, churches, and communities are affected. Useful technology should increase their ability to decide, evaluate, maintain, change, and, when necessary, refuse.

There is another question that no efficiency metric can answer. What is the saved time for? If artificial intelligence gives a missionary five hours back, that capacity can disappear into more email, more reports, and more output. Or it can become more language learning, more hospitality, more mentoring, more rest, more prayer, more time with people.

The technology creates capacity. Stewardship determines what the capacity becomes. The same is true of the Church as a whole. Perhaps the coming years will bring far better translation, more capable agents, stronger local models, and forms of assistance this book has not anticipated. None of that would change the deepest question.

Will Christians be faithful with what they have received? Someone will still need to teach. Someone will still need to welcome. Someone will still need to forgive. Someone will still need to stay when ministry becomes difficult. Someone will still need to cross a cultural boundary and become a learner.

Someone will still need to pray, send, give, serve, and bear witness. Artificial intelligence may help that person enormously. It cannot perform their obedience. The tools have changed.

The call has not.

Go.
