# When Expertise Becomes Cheap

One of the deepest economic effects of AI may be the falling cost of certain kinds of expertise.

A person can now ask for help with programming, statistics, writing, design, translation, research, or administration and receive useful assistance almost instantly.

The quality varies.

Experts remain necessary.

But the threshold for attempting unfamiliar work has fallen.

This matters for mission because mission organizations often operate under severe resource constraints.

A small team may need skills that would normally require several employees.

AI can provide temporary assistance across domains.

A missionary can debug a script.

A pastor can design a simple database.

A translator can automate repetitive text processing.

A ministry administrator can analyze survey results.

A local leader can learn unfamiliar software conversationally.

These capabilities do not make everyone an expert.

They make expertise more accessible.

The strategic effect may be similar to what happened when search engines made information easier to access.

People still needed judgment.

But the cost of finding answers fell dramatically.

AI may lower the cost of transforming information into action.

This creates opportunities for underserved ministries.

Large churches and organizations can already hire specialists.

The greatest relative benefit may therefore go to small teams that previously had no access at all.

A mission startup with two people may build tools that once required a department.

A rural ministry may receive technical guidance without flying in a consultant.

A Bible translation team may create scripts for data processing without a full-time programmer.

Christians should intentionally direct this capability toward places the market neglects.

If AI merely makes wealthy organizations slightly more efficient, its missionary significance will be limited.

If it enables small churches, minority language communities, and under-resourced ministries to gain new capability, the effect could be substantial.

But cheap expertise creates another danger.

People may attempt tasks beyond their ability to evaluate.

A novice can generate software without understanding security.

A ministry can produce legal documents without legal review.

A missionary can perform statistical analysis without understanding methodology.

AI lowers the cost of production faster than it lowers the cost of responsibility.

The user therefore needs a new skill:

knowing when generated competence is enough and when a real expert is required.

Cheap expertise is powerful.

Wise escalation remains essential.

[^1]: AI assistance often lowers the entry cost of technical work without eliminating the need for expert review in high-stakes domains.
