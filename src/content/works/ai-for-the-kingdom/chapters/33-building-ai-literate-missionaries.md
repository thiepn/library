AI literacy will age badly if defined as knowing clever prompts. Interfaces will improve. Models will understand ordinary requests better. The durable competencies concern knowing what the technology is doing, how to evaluate it, and when not to trust it. Six competencies form the missionary curriculum.[^1]

## What AI Literacy Actually Means {#what-ai-literacy-actually-means}

> • L0 — Unaware.
>
> • L1 — Basic user.
>
> • L2 — Operational.
>
> • L3 — Advanced.

L2 is the appropriate target for most missionaries: independently able to design ordinary workflows, verify, classify data, judge delegation, and recognize escalation.

AI literacy should join, not displace, missionary preparation in theology, culture, language, security, and ministry practice.

The technology is most useful when the worker already understands the ministry problem.

A person who does not know what faithful Bible interpretation requires cannot supervise an AI Bible-study workflow merely by learning better prompts.

## Six Competencies {#six-competencies}

The missionary understands enough to distinguish generation, retrieval, tools, agents, cloud processing, and local processing.

They do not need transformer mathematics. They need to predict common failure. The missionary can define the task, input, intended output, review point, and delegated authority.

Good AI work begins before the prompt. The missionary can distinguish a claim from a source, check quotations, verify statistics, and escalate domain questions.

Verification is one of the highest-leverage AI skills because it protects many other uses.

> • The missionary recognizes public, confidential, sensitive ministry, and high-risk information.
>
> • They know that convenience does not justify disclosure.
>
> • The missionary distinguishes asking, drafting, automating, and authorizing external action.
>
> • Capability does not automatically justify authority.
>
> • The missionary can ask whether AI is increasing competence or only dependence.

They periodically test critical abilities without assistance where those abilities remain necessary for responsible supervision.

> L2 is the normal operational target—not a badge of spiritual maturity.

## Competence Is Distributed {#competence-is-distributed}

AI competence is better tested through scenarios than definitions. A trainee receives a sensitive field report and is asked to design a summarization workflow. Another must verify a generated statistic. Another must decide whether an agent should receive send permission.

The assessment reveals judgment. Not every missionary needs advanced technical skill. Organizations can distribute expertise. Most workers target L2 Operational competence. A smaller number of L3 specialists support governance, security, evaluation, and mission-specific innovation.[^2]

## Formation Before Tool Mastery {#formation-before-tool-mastery}

> • AI competence cannot compensate for missionary immaturity.
>
> • The larger order remains:
>
> • Character → Biblical and Missionary Competence → Christian Community and Accountability → Technological Competence
>
> • Chapter 38 will integrate that hierarchy into the whole missionary life.
>
> • This chapter owns the training curriculum only.

Because capabilities change, training should teach update habits: read release notes selectively, test workflows, compare representative cases, and revisit assumptions.

The goal is not to chase every new model. It is to notice when a technological change materially alters a mission decision.

One of the highest AI competencies is knowing when one's own evaluation capacity ends.

Escalation is not failure. A missionary who recognizes that a security, translation, or legal question needs a specialist demonstrates mature literacy.
