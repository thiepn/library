# What Should We Delegate to AI?

The central governance question is not whether to use AI.

It is what to delegate.

A useful delegation framework considers three variables:

reversibility,

stakes,

and responsibility.

Low-stakes, reversible tasks are easy candidates for delegation.

Formatting text.

Generating ideas.

Categorizing documents.

Drafting routine messages.

Creating practice exercises.

Errors are inexpensive and easily corrected.

As stakes rise, human review should increase.

Translation drafts need review.

Research summaries need source checking.

Public theological material needs accountable editors.

Security decisions need specialists.

Some responsibilities should remain human even if AI becomes technically capable.

Pastoral discipline.

Ordination.

Moral accountability.

Consent.

Final decisions affecting vulnerable people.

The reason is not that machines will never produce useful recommendations.

The reason is that certain responsibilities belong to persons and communities.

A church elder cannot say, "The algorithm decided," and escape accountability.

Organizations should therefore classify AI use according to autonomy.

Level 0: no AI.

Level 1: AI suggests; human creates the final output.

Level 2: AI drafts; human reviews every result.

Level 3: AI acts within narrow rules; humans audit.

Level 4: AI acts autonomously in consequential areas.

Most ministry applications should remain in the middle levels.

High autonomy should be rare where people, money, doctrine, reputation, or security are affected.

The goal is appropriate delegation, not maximum automation.

Good governance keeps responsibility visible.

[^1]: Autonomy classifications are useful only when paired with concrete definitions of what the system can access, modify, publish, or decide.
