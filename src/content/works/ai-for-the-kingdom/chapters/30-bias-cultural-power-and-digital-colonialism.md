# Bias, Cultural Power, and Digital Colonialism

Artificial intelligence reflects the data and institutions that shape it.

Languages with large digital footprints receive more representation.

Cultures that dominate global media influence training data heavily.

Commercial priorities determine which users receive the best tools.

These realities create missionary concerns.

A model may present Western assumptions as universal.

It may describe minority religions through outsider categories.

It may produce stereotypes about ethnic groups.

It may perform poorly on names, dialects, or customs absent from its training.

Missionaries should not treat generated cultural explanations as neutral.

Bias does not always mean intentional prejudice.

It often means uneven representation.

A system trained mostly on English-language Christian material may explain theology through categories unfamiliar to another tradition or culture.

A translation model may push minority-language expression toward the grammar of dominant languages.

A recommendation system may privilege resources from large institutions.

This can become a form of digital colonialism when technology causes local communities to depend on categories, platforms, and content controlled elsewhere.

Christian mission has its own history of cultural mistakes.

Missionaries have sometimes confused the gospel with their own culture.

AI could accelerate the same error at scale.

The solution is local participation.

Build with communities, not merely for them.

Invite local believers to evaluate outputs.

Support local authorship.

Develop terminology collaboratively.

Preserve local language data responsibly.

Create systems that can incorporate local knowledge rather than forcing everyone into global defaults.

The Church should also invest in underserved languages even when commercial incentives are weak.

A language spoken by fifty thousand people may never become a profitable AI market.

Those fifty thousand people still matter.

Mission has always gone where markets do not.

The AI age should be no different.

Technology becomes missionary when it serves people who are economically easy to ignore.

[^1]: “Digital colonialism” is used here to describe patterns in which technological infrastructure, data, standards, or cultural assumptions become externally controlled in ways that reduce local agency.
