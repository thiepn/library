# Translation and Localization

Translation is one of the most obvious applications of artificial intelligence in mission.

Christian organizations produce enormous amounts of content: training manuals, Bible studies, websites, newsletters, theological articles, discipleship materials, videos, subtitles, policies, curricula, and software interfaces.

Historically, translating these resources into many languages required significant time and money.

AI can reduce the cost of first-pass translation dramatically.

A ministry can translate a draft into multiple languages within minutes.

The result may not be publication-ready.

But it can provide a starting point that a human translator edits rather than creating everything from zero.

This changes what becomes economically possible.

A small ministry may be able to localize resources that previously existed only in English.

A training program might reach ten language communities instead of two.

A missionary team could maintain multilingual documentation without a large translation department.

But translation is not localization.

A sentence can be linguistically accurate and culturally inappropriate.

Examples, metaphors, humor, illustrations, measurements, social assumptions, images, and tone may need adaptation.

Consider a discipleship lesson that uses American football as an illustration.

A literal translation may preserve the words while losing the meaning.

A financial stewardship course built around banking systems may need major adaptation in a cash-based economy.

A marriage seminar written for one cultural setting may carry assumptions about family structure that do not transfer directly.

Localization asks a broader question:

How should this resource live within another culture?

AI can help generate alternatives.

It can suggest culturally relevant examples, adapt reading levels, convert measurements, explain idioms, and reorganize material.

But these suggestions require local review.

The most reliable localization process includes people from the target community.

They understand what sounds natural.

They know which illustrations work.

They recognize theological terms that carry unintended meaning.

They can identify when a resource feels foreign even though the grammar is correct.

AI should therefore support localizers rather than bypass them.

A good workflow might include:

1. Human-authored source material.
2. AI-assisted draft translation.
3. Native-speaker revision.
4. AI-assisted consistency checking.
5. Theological review where required.
6. Local field testing.
7. Final publication.

This process uses AI where it is strong: speed, pattern comparison, terminology consistency, and draft generation.

It uses humans where they are indispensable: cultural judgment, theological responsibility, and lived language competence.

Organizations should also maintain translation memory and terminology databases.

AI becomes more useful when it receives approved vocabulary.

A mission agency can provide preferred translations for theological terms, organizational names, biblical names, and technical language.

This reduces inconsistency across documents.

For low-resource languages, these curated resources may become especially valuable.

A carefully built glossary can improve both human and machine translation.

Another issue is copyright.

Ministries should not assume that because an AI system can translate a resource, they have permission to publish the translation.

Translation creates a derivative work in many legal systems.

Licensing must still be respected.

The same applies to Bible translations, books, songs, and training materials.

Technical possibility does not remove legal or ethical responsibility.

Finally, ministries should consider whether translation itself is the best strategy.

Sometimes imported resources should not be translated at all.

Local Christians may be better served by creating original material within their own context.

AI could support this shift too.

Instead of translating a Western discipleship course into another language, local leaders might use AI to organize, edit, illustrate, and format resources they write themselves.

This represents a healthier goal.

Technology should not merely accelerate content export from powerful Christian centers.

It should strengthen local Christian authorship.

Translation expands access.

Localization builds relevance.

Local creation builds ownership.

AI can serve all three when used carefully.

[^1]: Translation quality should be evaluated separately from fluency; polished prose can still contain semantic, theological, legal, or cultural errors.
