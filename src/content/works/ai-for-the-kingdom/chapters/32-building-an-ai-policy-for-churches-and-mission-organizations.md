# Building an AI Policy for Churches and Mission Organizations

Organizations need clear expectations before problems occur.

A useful AI policy does not need to be long.

It should answer practical questions.

Which tools are approved?

What data may be submitted?

Which uses require human review?

What must be disclosed?

Which decisions cannot be automated?

How are errors reported?

How are generated sources verified?

Mission organizations should classify data.

Public information may be safe for broad tools.

Internal information may require approved accounts.

Confidential information may require private systems.

Highly sensitive field information may be prohibited from AI processing entirely.

The policy should also address content.

Generated public material should have a named human owner.

Statistics and quotations should be verified.

Synthetic media should not mislead audiences.

Theological material should receive appropriate review.

Policies should remain understandable.

If staff cannot remember the rules, the policy will not shape behavior.

Training matters more than paperwork.

Organizations should teach why the boundaries exist.

AI policy should also be reviewed regularly.

Capabilities and vendor practices change quickly.

A policy written once and forgotten will become obsolete.

The goal is not bureaucracy.

It is shared wisdom.

A good policy gives people freedom to use helpful tools while protecting the mission from predictable harm.

[^1]: Effective policy should distinguish data sensitivity, action authority, and publication risk instead of treating every AI use as equivalent.
