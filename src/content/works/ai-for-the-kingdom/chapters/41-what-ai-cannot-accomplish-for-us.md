The deepest limits of artificial intelligence are not limits of output. They are limits of delegation. This chapter does not need to prove that machines can never reason, create, understand, or become conscious. The book's conclusion survives whatever future capabilities emerge.[^1]

Five responsibilities remain ours. Claims about what machines can never do are vulnerable because technical capability changes and philosophical debates remain unresolved.

The book's Christian boundary is stronger when framed around responsibility. Even if a future machine reasons in ways current systems do not, Christians remain responsible for whether they obey Christ, how churches exercise authority, and how they love neighbors.

At the end of the book, the decisive test becomes simple. Did the technology make Christians more capable of faithful mission while preserving truth, responsibility, community, and local agency? If yes, use it gratefully. If not, capability alone is not enough.

AI can explain a command, translate it, apply it, and remind us of it.

None of that becomes our obedience. Forgiveness still requires the Christian to forgive. Generosity requires someone to give. Reconciliation requires people to seek peace.

The correct answer does not perform the act. AI can communicate Christian teaching remarkably well. The Church remains the community called to bear witness to Christ. Technology can mediate witness. It cannot turn the witnessing Church into an unnecessary layer between religious information and the world.

Tools can mediate obedience. A calendar reminds someone to visit. A translator helps a missionary speak. An agent schedules a meeting.

Mediation is not replacement. The Christian still owns the act as part of a life before God.

Christian witness has propositional content, but the New Testament also presents a witnessing community whose life, suffering, unity, generosity, and holiness bear testimony.

AI can communicate the propositions. The Church remains responsible for the communal life.

AI can inform teaching, discipline, safeguarding, leadership, and pastoral judgment. Accountable Christian communities remain responsible for what they do. “The model recommended it” cannot become an escape from responsibility. AI can facilitate action. It cannot make convenience identical with love. Faithfulness still takes the form of Christians showing up, keeping promises, serving, remaining, telling the truth, and carrying burdens.

Artificial intelligence habituates users to immediate response: ask, receive, revise, ask again. Christian life does not operate according to that interface. God is not a model. Prayer is not prompting. Providence is not optimization. Silence is not necessarily system failure.

Mission cannot become a closed engineering system in which sufficiently advanced inputs reliably produce spiritual outcomes.

That is enough.

The book does not need a stronger claim about what AI is. It needs a clear claim about what the Church is called to do.

AI can create an expectation that every question deserves an immediate response. Christian theology includes mystery, lament, waiting, and unanswered prayer. Faithfulness sometimes consists in remaining before God without a generated resolution.

[^1]: The chapter’s final limits are theological and ecclesial claims rather than technical forecasts; compare Kilner, *Dignity and Destiny: Humanity in the Image of God*; Flett, *The Witness of God: The Trinity, Missio Dei, Karl Barth, and the Nature of Christian Community*
