An organization may look strong on paper. The next question is what life inside it actually feels like. Your ordinary experience will usually be shaped more directly by a field leader, team, local church, funding rhythm, child's school, meetings, administrative demands, and recurring small decisions than by the global brand.

# Chapter 8 — The Agency You Actually Live With

You can agree with an organization's statement of faith without enjoying your team. You can admire global leadership while struggling under your field supervisor. You can trust its finances while discovering that local ministry relationships are weak.

This is why due diligence eventually has to become local.

## The Team Is Not a Footnote

Mission organizations use *team* in different ways. One team may plan ministry together, meet frequently, share responsibilities, and make decisions collectively. Another may consist of workers pursuing mostly independent ministries under one regional structure.

Neither model is automatically better. The difference matters.

Before joining, ask what being a member of **this** team actually requires: how often it meets, which responsibilities are shared, how decisions are made, how much freedom workers have, what social expectations exist, and what happens when people disagree.

The phrase *team-based mission* is too vague to answer those questions.

## Meet the Supervisor

The person who recruits you may have little authority over your life after deployment. The supervisor may influence role, priorities, language learning, evaluation, leave, conflict, and reassignment.

If you know the likely supervisor, speak with them before commitment. Ask:

- How do you make decisions when the team does not agree?
- What would cause you to tell a missionary they are not doing well?
- How do you give corrective feedback?
- What part of your leadership style do team members find difficult?

Then talk with someone who has actually worked under them.

A good supervisor does not need to be your favorite personality. The deeper questions concern clarity, fairness, correction, adaptability, and appeal.

## Can Bad News Travel Upward?

Healthy teams need truthful information. A plan may fail. A worker may be overwhelmed. A local pastor may disagree. Someone may believe the team's statistics are overstated.

Research on psychological safety and organizational voice suggests that people's willingness to speak is influenced by whether they expect interpersonal or organizational costs. Missionary conflict research likewise shows that team conflict is not incidental to long-term service.[^9][^12]

The useful question is not whether every conversation feels comfortable. Truthful disagreement can be uncomfortable. Ask instead: **Can a worker raise a meaningful concern without inappropriate retaliation?**

Ask for examples. “We value open communication” is weaker evidence than “Here is a serious disagreement that happened and how it was handled.”

## Conflict Does Not Prove You Chose Wrong

Mission teams contain different personalities, nationalities, denominations, expectations, and histories. Conflict should be expected.

Investigate how the team handles direct reconciliation, mediation, escalation, outside help, and reassignment. Healthy systems should preserve both perseverance and appropriate boundaries. Not every difficult relationship requires transfer. Not every working relationship can or should continue indefinitely.

Christian maturity requires more discernment than either “never leave” or “leave whenever it is hard.”

## The Local Church Relationship Matters

The field team should not become the missionary's entire Christian world.

Ask what local church you will belong to, who provides pastoral leadership, where you receive fellowship beyond the occupational team, whether local leaders influence ministry, and what happens when local Christians disagree with the expatriate plan.

A mission organization can unintentionally become employer, church, friendship network, housing system, spiritual mentor, and counseling gateway all at once. The more total the institution becomes, the more important independent relationships become.

## Local Christians Must Be Able to Correct the Missionary

Cross-cultural competence is not demonstrated by memorizing cultural facts. A more demanding test is whether local Christians can successfully change the missionary's understanding.

Ask experienced workers what they believed in their first year that they now think was wrong. Ask field leadership what the team has changed because local Christians challenged the approach.

Local believers are not infallible. Neither are expatriates. The goal is a ministry culture in which receiving-context Christians are genuine leaders, theologians, partners, and decision-makers—not only cultural advisers for foreign decisions.

## Keep Relationships Outside the Agency

Maintaining trusted relationships outside organizational authority can preserve perspective. Your sending church is especially important, and so may be close Christian friends, mentors, professional peers, or counselors.

The mission organization may become one of the most important Christian communities in your life. It should not need to become your entire identity.

## Interview More Than One Position

If possible, speak with:

- a newer worker: “What surprised you after arrival?”
- an experienced worker: “What matters now that you did not understand when you joined?”
- a team leader: “What causes otherwise good missionaries to struggle here?”
- a local Christian leader: “What are foreign workers from this organization like to work with?”
- a former worker: “What do you wish you had understood before joining?”

No one answer is final. Look for patterns.

Healthy teams often look unremarkable: supervisors keep meetings, give correction, protect language time, follow policy, admit mistakes; teams resolve enough conflict to keep working; local churches are listened to. These things rarely produce dramatic mission stories, which is one reason they are useful evidence.

The next chapter tests that ordinary reliability directly.

# Chapter 9 — The Tuesday Test

Mission is often imagined through unusual moments: the application interview, commissioning Sunday, departure gate, first gospel conversation in another language, first baptism.

Long-term ministry is mostly built out of ordinary days.

One useful tool is therefore the **Tuesday Test**: What will an ordinary Tuesday look like five years after you arrive?

It is a practical tool, not a biblical rule. Its purpose is to replace imagined missionary life with a concrete picture.

## Ask for an Ordinary Week

Speak with someone doing the role you are considering. Ask how much time goes to language study, direct ministry, preparation, meetings, administration, travel, supporter communication, household tasks, local relationships, and rest.

A job description may say “church planting” while an ordinary week includes large amounts of language study, hospitality, transportation, meetings, and administration. Those tasks may be completely legitimate. They reveal what the job actually is.

## Language Learning May Be the Work for a While

New missionaries can arrive eager for visible ministry. A responsible agency may ask them to spend substantial time learning language, culture, local history, and relationships.

The practical question is whether the field protects what headquarters says it values. If language is “essential” but ministry pressure repeatedly causes workers to abandon it early, the operating system conflicts with the training system.

Ask how many hours are protected, who evaluates progress, when ministry responsibilities increase, and what happens when a worker struggles.

## Administration Is Still Work

Missionaries may handle expense reports, visas, project budgets, statistics, safeguarding procedures, travel planning, and donor correspondence. It is easy to call all of this bureaucracy and imagine that faithful missionaries would spend every hour in direct ministry.

Some administration protects money, people, legal status, and accountability. The useful question is whether the organization minimizes avoidable administrative burden while preserving necessary controls.

## Meetings Reveal Operating Culture

Team planning, prayer, regional coordination, one-to-one supervision, project meetings, and local church meetings can all be useful. They can also consume enormous amounts of time.

Ask how many meetings occur and why each exists. An ordinary week is shaped by accumulated small obligations.

## Support Raising May Follow You Overseas

If your agency uses individual support raising, ongoing newsletters, church visits, calls, and support rebuilding may remain part of the role. That can be healthy partnership, but it still consumes time.

Ask a current worker how much attention supporter communication requires in a normal year.

## The Household Lives the Assignment Too

If you are married, your spouse is not an accessory to your calling. If you have children, they are not logistical units attached to your missionary profile. A missionary aspiration does not suspend ordinary Christian responsibility toward family.

That also does not mean family comfort becomes the highest Christian good. Missionary families may knowingly accept distance, disruption, modest housing, cultural adaptation, and difficult schooling choices. The issue is whether those costs are understood and carried responsibly rather than spiritualized away.

Ask what is expected of each spouse, whether both are formal members, what happens after childbirth or during caregiving seasons, whether part-time service is possible, and how family needs enter field decisions.

## Children Need More Than “There Is a School”

Parents should investigate actual schooling options, language of education, transitions between systems, safeguarding, special educational needs, healthcare, and university transition.

One hard but important question is: **What happens if the field remains excellent for ministry but becomes unsustainable for one of our children?**

A healthy organization should be able to discuss that without turning family needs into a verdict on parental faith.

## Single Missionaries Need Their Own Questions

Family policy does not automatically cover single workers well. Singleness may affect housing, travel, security, friendship, loneliness, team expectations, and boundaries.

Ask single missionaries what makes long-term life in the field sustainable or difficult for them. An organization can serve married families well and structure life poorly for singles, or the reverse.

## Loneliness Is Not Simply a Personal Weakness

The 2025 North American Launch Survey identified loneliness, family distance, support raising, role ambiguity, and lack of clear direction among recurring concerns for aspiring missionaries.[^1] Other missionary and humanitarian research likewise highlights the relevance of social and organizational support under stress.[^13]

No agency can guarantee that a missionary will never be lonely. Organizations do influence team placement, mentoring, community, home assignment, and access to care.

Ask: **Who will know me well enough to notice when I am struggling?**

## Home Assignment Is More Than Rest

Returning to the sending country can involve family, donor meetings, church visits, medical appointments, training, administration, and reverse culture adjustment.

Ask how often home assignment occurs, how much speaking or fundraising is expected, whether housing is available, and how much actual rest is protected.

## Watch for Role Creep

Long-term missionaries often accumulate responsibilities: team leadership, mentoring, administration, short-term-team hosting, finance, or recruitment. Growth can be appropriate. The danger appears when organizations mainly add responsibilities and rarely remove them.

Ask experienced workers what responsibilities have accumulated and what leadership deliberately removed as their roles expanded.

## What Counts as Doing Well?

An ordinary Tuesday is shaped by what the organization rewards.

If the first year is language-focused, does evaluation actually protect language learning? If local leadership is the goal, is the missionary encouraged when local Christians increasingly make decisions? If ministry is slow, can the worker report that honestly?

No metric captures Christian faithfulness completely, but unclear expectations create problems of their own.

## Ask Each Household Member a Different Question

Missionary families can accidentally make a collective decision through one person's enthusiasm. A stronger process gives different members space to describe the same assignment from their own position.

A spouse may need to answer not only “Do I support this calling?” but “What life am I personally being asked to live?” A teenager may see schooling, friendship, and repeated transition differently from a parent. A single worker may need to ask whether team structures unintentionally assume every adult has a spouse providing built-in companionship or practical support.

This does not mean children exercise the same decision authority as parents or that every family member must feel equally excited. It means the household's actual life should enter the discernment process rather than appearing after the ministry plan is fixed.

## Reentry Begins Before You Leave

Home assignment and permanent return become easier to understand when they are planned before crisis. Ask who pays travel, what housing options exist, what the organization expects from supporters and churches during the return, and whether children moving between educational systems receive any guidance.

For long-term workers, retirement also belongs somewhere in the lifecycle. An agency does not need to control a missionary's retirement, but candidates should understand whether pension arrangements, reentry, and eventual separation from organizational systems have been considered.

The Tuesday Test asks whether ordinary life is workable. The next test asks what happens when several parts of that ordinary system break at once.

# Chapter 10 — The Bad-Year Test

Imagine the first years go reasonably well. Then one year changes everything.

Support drops. A child's schooling becomes difficult. Ministry stalls. Your relationship with the team leader deteriorates. A political change threatens the visa. Your spouse is exhausted.

None of these circumstances requires institutional wrongdoing. Missionary life can simply become hard.

The useful question is not “Will anything bad happen?” It is: **What happens when several parts of the system are under pressure at the same time?**

Call this the **Bad-Year Test**. Like the Tuesday Test, it is a prudential exercise rather than a biblical formula.

## Crisis Reveals Priority

Most organizations can describe values when those values are inexpensive. Pressure creates conflict between good things: ministry continuity versus family health; reputation versus truthful disclosure; worker autonomy versus security; budget pressure versus adequate care; team stability versus removing an unhealthy leader.

Response reveals how the organization orders priorities.

Ask leadership: **What is a significant mistake this organization has made, and what changed because of it?**

An organization old enough to have substantial ministry history should normally be able to identify failed strategies, leadership conflicts, financial problems, or other institutional mistakes without revealing confidential details. A history presented as uninterrupted success deserves more investigation.

## What Happens When Ministry Fails?

Candidates can become so identified with a role that failure feels like a verdict on their calling. Organizations can reinforce this if unsuccessful projects are quietly hidden.

Ask whether ministries have been closed because a strategy was not working, and how that decision was made. Failure may require persistence, redesign, transfer, or closure. Continuing every failed project forever is not perseverance. Stopping every difficult project quickly is not wisdom.

The key is whether truthful evaluation is possible.

## What Happens When You Disagree With Leadership?

Conflict with a supervisor becomes more consequential during a bad year. A responsible organization should have a path between “just submit” and “leave immediately”: direct conversation, mediation, regional review, formal appeal, or reassignment as appropriate.

A real appeal process does not mean the worker always gets the outcome they want. It means authority can be reviewed.

## Security Decisions May Override Preference

During a crisis, an organization may order workers to relocate, evacuate, stop public ministry, or change communication practices. Centralized authority can be legitimate when the organization carries responsibility and has security information the worker does not possess.

Candidates should know before the crisis who decides evacuation, whether workers can leave earlier, what happens if they refuse an order, and how family needs enter the decision.

## Evacuation Is More Than a Plane Ticket

Forced departure can mean sudden loss of home, friends, ministry, possessions, and future plans. Research on missionary evacuation describes relational and psychological disruption following abrupt departures.[^14]

Crisis response should therefore include more than extraction logistics. Ask what happens after return, whether debriefing or counseling is available, and what occurs if the worker can never return.

## Health Can Change the Assignment

Physical or psychological health may alter service. Two simplistic rules should be rejected:

> If you were truly called, you should remain.

and

> Any serious distress proves you should leave.

Health questions may require professional care, reduced workload, temporary return, reassignment, or long-term departure. A mature organization can make those decisions without turning medical limitation into a spiritual verdict.

## Family Needs Can Change the Assignment

A parent becomes seriously ill. A child develops needs that cannot be met in the field. A marriage requires substantial attention.

These changes do not automatically determine one outcome. They do mean the original decision may need reconsideration. Christian perseverance is not identical to institutional immobility.

## Reassignment Should Be Understood Before It Happens

Organizations may change roles because of visas, strategy, leadership needs, family circumstances, or emerging competence. Ask who can initiate reassignment, whether worker consent is required, how the sending church participates, and what happens when the proposed role is a poor fit.

You are not negotiating permanent control over the future. You are understanding how major changes are made.

## Leaving Is Not Automatically Failure

Missionaries sometimes remain because leaving feels spiritually impossible after years of preparation, support raising, public commissioning, and identity investment. Decision research on sunk costs and escalation of commitment helps explain why prior investment can weigh heavily on future decisions.[^15]

One useful diagnostic is the **Zero-Sunk-Cost Question**: If you had invested nothing and knew everything you know today, would you still enter this arrangement?

The question does not tell you what to do. Sometimes remaining is faithful. Sometimes changing course is. It helps reveal when past investment is doing too much work in the current decision.

## How Does the Organization Treat People Who Leave?

Exit behavior is unusually revealing. Ask former workers how donors were informed, benefits handled, concerns heard, and relationships treated.

An organization can disagree strongly with a departing worker and still treat them with Christian integrity. Departure should not automatically become excommunication from a ministry family.

## Reentry Is Part of Sending

Long-term sending should include thought about return. Reentry can involve reverse culture shock, changed relationships, employment, schooling, and identity disruption.[^16]

Ask what debriefing, transition help, pastoral support, or practical assistance exists when someone returns permanently.

## The Bad-Year Test Does Not Promise Protection From Suffering

The point is not to select an agency that prevents grief, persecution, financial loss, or disappointment. Christian mission can be costly.

The distinction is between suffering an organization could not reasonably prevent and harm the organization predictably worsened through negligence, coercion, dishonesty, or inadequate systems.

Would you trust this organization if leadership changed, ministry failed, finances deteriorated, your family needed help, you disagreed seriously, or you had to leave?

## The Organization's Response to Departure Matters Even When the Worker Was Wrong

Former-worker interviews can become unfair if every departure is treated as proof that the organization failed. Sometimes a missionary leaves after misconduct, persistent poor performance, or unwillingness to follow legitimate policy. Organizations need authority to discipline and, where necessary, terminate membership or employment.

The due-diligence question is therefore not “Did everyone who left feel good about it?” That standard is impossible. Ask instead whether the process was intelligible, whether allegations were investigated appropriately, whether the worker could respond or appeal where appropriate, and whether communication with churches or supporters remained truthful.

A healthy organization can make an unpopular decision. It should still be able to exercise authority without unnecessary humiliation, retaliation, or distortion.

## Ask About Institutional Memory

Another useful question is what happens after a bad year is over. Does the organization learn only at the individual level—“that leader was difficult”—or does it preserve lessons in policy, training, supervision, or governance?

Institutional learning is one reason written records, incident reviews, and historical honesty matter. The goal is not bureaucratic accumulation. It is preventing every new generation from rediscovering the same failure at somebody else's expense.

If the only evidence comes from workers whose stories are going well, you do not yet know much about the system under pressure.
