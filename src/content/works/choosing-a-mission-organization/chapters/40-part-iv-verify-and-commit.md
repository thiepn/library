You have examined the work, organization, and life you may enter. One problem remains: most of what you know was told to you by someone. The final stage is therefore epistemic. What evidence deserves trust, and when have you collected enough to stop investigating and commit?

# Chapter 11 — Build Evidence, Not Impressions

Most candidates begin with impressions. The organization appears serious. The missionaries seem mature. The website is professional. A pastor speaks highly of them.

Impressions matter. They are not yet the same as evidence strong enough to support a major commitment.

Ask: **How do I know what I think I know about this organization?**

## Start With What the Organization Says

Official material is an important first layer. Review the statement of faith, candidate handbook, financial information, safeguarding policy, member-care material, field descriptions, and role expectations where relevant.

First-party material is especially useful for stated beliefs, formal systems, and official commitments. Its limitation is obvious: the organization chose what to publish. That does not make the material deceptive. It means it answers only part of the question.

## Request Documents for Consequential Claims

Use the **Documentation Test** where consequences are substantial.

If someone says you are insured, ask what the coverage includes. If they say there is an appeal process, ask where it is described. If they describe a particular support arrangement, ask for the policy.

You do not need paperwork for every conversational detail. Use documentation where misunderstanding would matter later.

## Speak With Current Workers

Current workers can describe what policies feel like in practice. Ask concrete questions:

- What surprised you after arriving?
- What is better than expected?
- What frustrates good missionaries here?
- What does headquarters misunderstand about the field?
- When did the organization care for you especially well?
- What happens when you disagree with leadership?

Specific incidents are more informative than adjectives such as “healthy” or “supportive.”

## Speak With Former Workers

Former workers may understand exit, conflict, and transition in ways current workers do not.

Ask why they left, whether leaving was their choice, how leadership responded, what they discovered only after joining, whether they would join again, and what criticism of the organization they believe would be unfair.

That last question is useful because it tests whether the person can still distinguish real weakness from total condemnation.

Former workers are not automatically objective. Current workers are not automatically propaganda. Both are evidence sources requiring interpretation.

## Speak With Receiving-Context Christians

Where appropriate and safe, local Christian leaders add a perspective candidates often miss.

Ask whether the agency listens, whether local leaders possess meaningful authority, what foreign workers are like in ordinary relationships, and whether expatriate ministry strengthens or crowds out local leadership.

Do not reduce local Christians to cultural informants. Ask about theology, leadership, decision rights, and power.

## Look for Convergence

The strongest evidence often occurs when different source types broadly align.

Suppose official policy describes a route for complaints to bypass direct leadership. Current workers know how it works. A former worker says they used it. Leadership can explain a case where the system changed an outcome. That is much stronger than the policy alone.

A useful evidence stack is:

1. stated values;
2. formal systems;
3. actual resource allocation;
4. current-worker experience;
5. former-worker experience;
6. local/partner testimony;
7. behavior under pressure.

You will rarely obtain all seven equally. The goal is reasonable convergence, not investigative omniscience.

## Give Adverse Events Serious Weight Without Letting Them Dominate Everything

Organizations are especially informative when values conflict with institutional self-interest. Leadership transitions, financial pressure, failed strategy, serious complaints, field closures, and worker departures can reveal what ordinary promotional material cannot.

History must still be used fairly. A serious failure decades ago should not automatically define current workers. Investigate response, reform, and current systems. Longevity itself is not proof of health.

## Read Mission Statistics Carefully

Agencies sometimes use global data to explain priorities. Ask which source, what year, what definition, and what the statistic actually measures.

Terms such as *unreached*, *unengaged*, and *frontier* are strategic categories. Major missions databases use different units and thresholds. Joshua Project and IMB, for example, can publish different counts of unreached people groups because their methodologies differ.[^17]

A number can be useful without being a timeless census.

Likewise, a widely repeated Lausanne estimate that about three percent of international missionaries serve among populations classified as unreached is based on older allocation data rather than a 2026 census.[^18]

The durable lesson is methodological: strategic data can inform stewardship; it cannot mechanically become an individual divine assignment.

## Internet Allegations Require Discipline

You may encounter serious criticism online. Do not dismiss it because it is negative. Do not accept it merely because it is vivid.

Ask what is alleged, what documentation exists, whether an independent investigation occurred, what remains disputed, and how the organization responded.

One serious allegation may justify further investigation. It does not automatically justify a sweeping public verdict. The standard for personal due diligence and the standard for publicly accusing a person or institution are not identical.

## Confidentiality Can Limit What You Learn

Sometimes an organization cannot tell you everything. Personnel matters, medical information, safeguarding cases, and restricted-field details may require confidentiality.

Instead of demanding sensitive facts, ask about process, accountability, and oversight. The inability to disclose everything is not the same as institutional concealment.

## Build the Best Case Both Ways

Due diligence can become structurally negative. After investigating weaknesses, deliberately write the strongest fair case for joining.

What does the organization do unusually well? Why have long-term workers stayed? When did leadership respond well under pressure? Which strengths fit the responsibility you are pursuing?

Then write the strongest case against joining.

The exercise does not require artificial balance. If evidence strongly favors one conclusion, let it. The purpose is to prevent one memorable negative or positive impression from carrying the whole decision.

## Evidence Needs a Stopping Point

At some stage you should be able to say:

- I understand the consequential claims.
- I checked the important ones.
- The organization is not perfect.
- The remaining uncertainty is acceptable.

If you cannot say that because major questions remain unresolved, keep investigating. If you cannot say it because you require complete certainty about every future possibility, the problem may no longer be inadequate information.

## Weight Evidence According to What It Can Actually Prove

Different evidence types answer different questions.

A statement of faith is strong evidence for what the organization officially confesses. It is weak evidence for whether one field leader behaves humbly. An audited financial statement is strong evidence for particular financial controls. It is weak evidence for member care. A former worker can provide strong evidence about their own experience. Their experience alone is weaker evidence for the global organization across every era and field.

This discipline prevents both promotional overclaiming and cynical overclaiming.

It also changes how you use reputation. A famous agency may deserve initial credibility because many churches know it and substantial systems exist. Reputation should lower neither the standard of verification nor the possibility of trust. A small or new agency may have less historical evidence; the right response is proportionate caution, not automatic rejection.

## What to Do When Sources Conflict

Suppose headquarters says workers receive strong field support, while two former workers describe isolation. Do not immediately average the answers or decide somebody is lying.

Ask whether the workers served in the same region, under the same leaders, during the same period. Ask whether policy changed. Ask current workers on the actual team. Contradiction often becomes more informative when you discover the level at which each statement is true.

Some conflicts will remain unresolved. Record them as unresolved rather than forcing a verdict. Residual uncertainty is part of the decision, not necessarily evidence that the organization is hiding something.

The final chapter turns the investigation back on the decision-maker.

# Chapter 12 — Decide Without Demanding Certainty

Research can improve a decision. It cannot make the decision for you.

Eventually you will have documents, conversations, concerns, strengths, and church counsel. Then the question returns: **Should I join?**

Discernment can fail in two opposite directions. One candidate commits too quickly because the opportunity feels right. Another investigates indefinitely because no institution feels safe enough.

Neither extreme is necessary.

## Your Decision Process Needs Due Diligence Too

You have spent most of the book evaluating the organization. Now evaluate yourself.

Ask:

- Why do I want this particular agency?
- Which people have influenced me most?
- What am I afraid of losing if I say no?
- Have I become so invested that changing direction feels humiliating?
- Am I demanding a level of certainty no institution can provide?

Human judgment is affected by attraction, belonging, prior investment, social pressure, optimism, and commitment escalation. Decision research does not mean your choice is secretly irrational. It means discernment benefits from structures that make self-correction easier.[^15]

## Keep Recruiter Trust at the Right Level

You may genuinely trust the recruiter. That does not personally prove every field is healthy, every supervisor wise, or every policy effective.

Separate trust in a person, institution, field, team, and supervisor. Confidence can grow at each level through relevant evidence.

## Keep Calling Language Correctable

Once you say, “God has called me to Organization X,” disconfirming evidence becomes psychologically harder to receive.

While the decision is genuinely open, careful language can preserve discernment: “I believe God may be leading me toward this work, and I am seriously exploring Organization X.”

That is not weak faith. It is honest about the level of certainty actually possessed.

## Define Red Lines Before Deep Investment

Early in the process, identify what would cause you to withdraw: material theological incompatibility, serious unresolved safeguarding concerns, significant dishonesty, or a clearly unsuitable supervisor, for example.

Do this before friendships, support commitments, public announcements, and departure plans make every boundary psychologically expensive to maintain.

Precommitment should not become rigidity. New information may show that a concern was misunderstood. Its purpose is simply to prevent every threshold from being quietly lowered as investment grows.

## The Zero-Sunk-Cost Question

If substantial new information appears after years of preparation, ask:

> **If I had invested nothing and knew everything I know today, would I still enter this arrangement?**

This is not a biblical command and does not erase legitimate commitments. It helps reveal when irrecoverable past investment is carrying too much weight in the present decision.

## Use a Red Team—and a Steelman

Ask a mature Christian who understands the situation to make the strongest case against joining. What are you overlooking? Which evidence is weakest? What failure scenario are you discounting?

Then do the opposite. Build the strongest fair case for joining.

Your sending church may be especially well positioned to help with both.

## Let Church Counsel Be Real

Do not involve the church merely as a rubber stamp. Give trusted pastors or elders permission to say, “You are moving too quickly,” “Fear may be driving this,” or “Another organization deserves consideration.”

Church counsel is not infallible. It should nevertheless be meaningful.

Proverbs commends the value of wise counsel (for example, Proverbs 15:22), and James directs believers who lack wisdom to ask God for it (James 1:5). Neither passage gives a mechanistic formula for discovering an agency. They do remind Christians that wise decisions need neither radical individualism nor confidence in technique alone.

## Count the Cost Without Worshiping Safety

Christian mission may involve distance, hardship, uncertainty, and danger. A due-diligence framework becomes distorted if it teaches candidates to choose whichever arrangement minimizes cost.

A dangerous field may still be the right field. A negligent organization is not made faithful merely because workers suffer more.

Count risk. Do not make safety the highest good.

## Fit Is Not Comfort

A candidate may label ordinary inconvenience “poor fit.” A healthy team may disagree with you. Language learning may be exhausting. Organizational processes may require patience. Good fit means the arrangement is workable for faithful service, not that it removes inconvenience.

Likewise, hardship after joining does not prove you chose incorrectly. A good decision can lead into suffering, disappointing ministry, or relational strain.

Due diligence improves the quality of the decision. It does not guarantee an easy outcome.

## Establish a Stopping Rule

A practical stopping rule might be:

**Proceed when:**

- no unresolved presumptive disqualifier remains;
- major material concerns have satisfactory answers;
- the organization can support the responsibility;
- the actual field, team, and supervisor have been investigated enough to be real rather than abstract;
- your church is substantially satisfied;
- the principal trade-offs are understood;
- remaining uncertainty is ordinary rather than negligent.

This is not a biblical formula. Adapt it to the significance of the decision.

## Then Commit

## Due Diligence Must Not Turn You Into a Consumer

A candidate can learn to ask so many questions that the organization begins to look like a product being purchased. That is another distortion.

You are not entitled to a frictionless ministry experience. A mission organization may legitimately require you to follow security rules you dislike, complete training you would not have chosen, accept correction, report finances carefully, or work within a team decision you did not prefer.

The relevant distinction is between **legitimate organizational authority** and authority that is unbounded, dishonest, coercive, or unable to be reviewed.

A good selection process therefore asks two symmetrical questions:

1. Is this organization trustworthy enough for me to join?
2. Am I prepared to become a trustworthy member of it?

The second question includes humility, truthfulness, teachability, financial integrity, appropriate submission, and willingness to do ordinary work that may never appear in a missionary story.

## Pray Without Using Prayer to Make Evidence Irrelevant

Christian discernment cannot be reduced to a matrix. Pray for wisdom. Search Scripture. Seek counsel. Pay attention to providential circumstances. But do not use a subjective sense of peace to make serious evidence irrelevant.

Likewise, do not treat an analytically strong process as a substitute for dependence on God. A spreadsheet cannot tell you the future. The purpose of due diligence is to help you act faithfully within creaturely limits, not to become sovereign over uncertainty.

Once you join, you should not remain in permanent candidacy mode.

You now owe the organization something too: truthfulness, legitimate obedience, teachability, financial integrity, contribution to team health, respect for local Christians, and willingness to seek reconciliation.

Due diligence does not make you a customer. You are becoming a participant.

The organization should be trustworthy enough to receive your commitment. You should aim to become a trustworthy member of it.

## Remain Teachable

Commitment is not blindness. Organizations change. You change. Fields change. Serious problems may later require reassessment.

But do not spend every ordinary disagreement asking whether another agency would be slightly better. The purpose of discernment is eventually to create stability for service.

Enough investigation. Enough evidence. Enough trust.

Then serve.
