A mission organization can feel right long before you know whether it is trustworthy.

You meet missionaries whose theology sounds like yours. They care about the same places you care about. The recruiter understands your church background. The organization offers a role that seems unusually well matched to your abilities. After several conversations, the feeling becomes difficult to ignore: *These are my people.*

Compatibility matters. Person–organization, person–job, team, and supervisor fit are distinct concepts in organizational research, and perceived fit strongly affects attraction to organizations.[^3] Missionary-specific research has likewise found that relational connection to agency personnel and perceived fit between gifts and agency work can influence agency choice.[^4]

But compatibility answers only one question: **Could I function well here?**

It does not answer another: **Should this organization receive my trust?**

## Qualification Before Compatibility

Suppose an organization fits you unusually well. Its theology matches your church. Its ministry strategy makes sense. Its team culture feels relational. But you discover that serious grievances can only be reported through the leader implicated in them, financial controls are unclear, and workers cannot explain how senior leadership can be challenged.

Strong compatibility does not rescue that decision.

Some problems are too serious to average away. This is why a simple agency score is dangerous. A safeguarding failure should not be averaged against excellent conference training, generous benefits, and a preferred location.

The book therefore uses three practical stages:

**Qualification** asks whether serious theological, integrity, governance, safeguarding, or truthfulness problems place the organization below a reasonable threshold for trust.

**Capability** asks whether the organization can actually support the particular work.

**Compatibility** then asks whether the theology, role, team, church relationship, finances, household situation, and context fit.

“Qualification” is not a scientific certification or a universal biblical checklist. It is shorthand for threshold-level concerns that should not be treated as ordinary preferences.

## Compatibility Can Amplify Weakness

A candidate who values sacrifice may feel at home on a team where everyone works unsustainable hours. A candidate frustrated by slow church processes may love a founder-led organization where decisions are fast and dissent rare. The fit can feel excellent precisely because the culture reinforces the candidate's own blind spot.

Likewise, a good organization can still be wrong for you. It may be financially transparent, accountable, doctrinally responsible, and competent, yet its team structure, support model, or ministry philosophy may be a poor fit for your church or family.

Declining a good organization is not an accusation against it.

## Four Kinds of Concern

Do not call everything a red flag.

**RED — Presumptive disqualifier.** A serious theological, integrity, or safety issue.

**ORANGE — Resolve before commitment.** A material uncertainty that needs a satisfactory answer.

**YELLOW — Investigate.** A signal requiring more information.

**GRAY — Trade-off or preference.** A difference that is not inherently healthy or unhealthy.

This is a decision aid, not a spiritual verdict. Its value lies in keeping a small preference from receiving the same weight as a serious integrity problem.

## Trust Is Still the Goal

A book about due diligence can drift into suspicion. Mission requires trust. You cannot serve for years while treating every leader as a potential adversary. The aim of investigation is not permanent skepticism. It is **informed trust**.

The difference is between:

> I trust them because they are Christians.

and:

> I trust them because their beliefs, systems, leaders, workers, finances, and responses to difficulty broadly align—and because people I trust have examined the relationship with me.

The second kind of trust is not less Christian. It is better grounded.

## Do Not Search for the Perfect Agency

Once investigation becomes serious, imperfection appears everywhere. One organization has stronger benefits but more bureaucracy. Another offers more freedom but less centralized support. One has exceptional language training but a long preparation pathway. Another deploys quickly but expects more learning under field supervision.

The task is not to prove that no better organization exists anywhere. It is to determine whether this sending arrangement is **sufficiently trustworthy, capable, and appropriate** for the responsibility before you.

That is a lower standard than perfection and a higher standard than enthusiasm.
