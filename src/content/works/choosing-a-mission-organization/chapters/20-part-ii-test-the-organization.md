Safeguarding and grievance systems show what happens when protecting people is expensive. Capability determines whether a trustworthy organization can actually support the work.

# Chapter 4 — Theology, Church, and Accountable Authority

Most Christian candidates know to read a mission organization's statement of faith. They should. If you are considering giving an organization years of ministry, you should understand what it believes about Scripture, God, Christ, salvation, the church, and mission.

But doctrinal compatibility is only the beginning of theological due diligence. The more difficult question is: **What happens when theology becomes organizational practice?**

An agency may affirm biblical authority and still use Scripture carelessly when leadership is challenged. It may affirm the local church while giving churches almost no meaningful role in missionary decisions. It may affirm servant leadership while concentrating practical authority in one unaccountable person.

Christian doctrine should therefore be examined both as confession and as organizational logic.

## Begin With the Gospel

Does the organization faithfully proclaim the Christian gospel?

This book works within a broadly historic evangelical framework: the authority and trustworthiness of Scripture; the Triune God; the full deity and humanity of Christ; His saving death and bodily resurrection; salvation by grace through faith; repentance and faith; the work of the Holy Spirit; the church; Christ's return, resurrection, and judgment; and the Great Commission.

Mission organizations may differ legitimately on many secondary issues. Operational excellence cannot compensate for a confused gospel, but not every secondary difference deserves equal weight.

Ask three questions:

1. What does the organization actually require?
2. What will you be expected to teach or practice?
3. Can you and your sending church participate without concealing real convictions?

If membership requires hiding material disagreement, the relationship is already structurally unhealthy.

## Theology Must Reach the Field

A global statement of faith can create the impression that theological alignment is settled. Field practice may introduce questions the statement never answers.

- What church is a missionary expected to join locally?
- What theological standards govern church-planting partnerships?
- How does the team handle disagreement with an established local church?
- Who resolves theological disagreement inside a team?

Theology operates through decisions. Ask not only what the organization believes, but what happens when belief creates a real disagreement.

## Christian Leadership Has Moral Boundaries

The New Testament gives substantial teaching about church leadership. Peter warns elders not to domineer over those entrusted to them but to be examples (1 Peter 5:1–4). Paul warns the Ephesian elders that danger can arise from within leadership itself (Acts 20:28–31).

These texts concern church leaders, not modern nonprofit executives, so they should not be turned into corporate bylaws. They do reveal durable Christian moral principles: spiritual leadership is not self-protecting power; character matters; entrusted people are not possessions; leaders remain accountable to God; internal leadership failure is possible.

A mission organization's exact corporate form is prudential. How Christian leaders use authority is not morally neutral.

## Agency and Church Are Not the Same Institution

A mission agency may train missionaries, appoint or employ them, administer funds, oversee field strategy, and supervise workers. That can give it substantial organizational authority.

It should not quietly become a complete substitute for the church.

The church gathers God's people, provides pastoral leadership, teaches Scripture, practices the ordinances according to its tradition, and embodies Christian community beyond occupational ministry. Missionary teams may pray, worship, study Scripture, and confess sin together. Those practices are good. They do not automatically make the agency a church.

This distinction matters when one leader becomes simultaneously supervisor, spiritual mentor, evaluator, housing decision-maker, and quasi-pastor. That combination may work under a mature leader. It also concentrates power.

Healthy organizations make it possible to distinguish: “My supervisor has legitimate authority over this work decision” from “My supervisor has divine authority to determine God's personal will for me.”

## Follow the Decision Rights

Instead of asking whether leadership is servant-hearted, identify what leaders can actually decide.

Who can appoint the executive leader? Who can remove them? Who sets compensation? Who investigates allegations? Who approves major strategy? Who controls significant funds? Who can overturn a field decision?

A founder-led organization deserves particular attention. Founders often possess unusual knowledge and legitimate influence. The question is whether the organization can still tell the founder “no.” If nobody can, accountability depends too heavily on personality.

Many organizations have boards. “Has a board” is not enough. Ask how board members are selected, whether they can challenge executives, whether they receive information from more than one source, and whether they can remove senior leadership.

Do not treat one national nonprofit model as biblical. Follow the function: can authority genuinely be reviewed?

## Succession Reveals Structure

Organizations often function differently while a respected founder or long-serving leader remains. Relationships can compensate for structural ambiguity. Succession exposes it.

Mission history repeatedly shows disputes over property, funds, appointments, field authority, and institutional direction when founding relationships no longer hold a structure together. One useful historical question is therefore: **Tell me about a significant leadership transition. What changed, and what did the organization learn?**

A healthy organization should not require one unusually gifted personality to remain healthy forever.

## Church Partnership Is Also an Authority Question

Ask both your church and the agency:

- Who determines whether you are ready?
- Who commissions you?
- Who provides pastoral oversight?
- What happens if your church believes you should return home?
- What happens if the agency has a doctrinal concern?
- What happens if church and agency disagree?

There may not be one universal answer. What matters is that nobody discovers the structure for the first time in crisis.

## Theological Change Is Possible

Adoniram and Ann Judson provide a useful historical illustration. They sailed under Congregationalist sponsorship, reconsidered baptism during the journey, embraced believer's baptism, and could no longer remain in the same denominational relationship. Their missionary commitment continued even though the sending structure changed.[^5]

The lesson here is organizational, not an argument over baptism: missionaries continue studying Scripture, and material convictions may change. An organization needs a way to handle serious theological change honestly. Charitable transfer or resignation may sometimes be the faithful outcome. Concealment should not be the price of institutional continuity.

## Accountable Authority Is Still Real Authority

Healthy authority can make decisions, enforce standards, correct workers, remove people who should not remain, and respond quickly in emergencies. What makes authority accountable is that it is defined, bounded, reviewable, and subject to legitimate higher authority.

A mission team without authority can become chaotic. A mission team without accountable authority can become dangerous.

## Ask How Leadership Learns It Is Wrong

A leadership structure can look impressive while remaining poor at self-correction. One of the most useful governance questions is therefore not “Who is in charge?” but **“How does leadership learn that it has made a serious mistake?”**

Possible mechanisms include staff feedback, board review, external audits, field councils, formal appeals, local-church input, safeguarding channels, or outside advisers. The precise structure varies. The important feature is that truth does not depend entirely on the willingness of the most powerful person to recognize their own error.

This question is especially valuable for spiritually admired leaders. Missionary history contains leaders of extraordinary faith, courage, and effectiveness. Admiration should not require institutional dependence on a person's virtue alone. Christian theology gives good reason to take character seriously and equally good reason to remember that every leader remains fallible.

Ask for a concrete example: **When did leadership reverse an important decision because people below them or outside the immediate leadership circle persuaded them they were wrong?**

If the organization has no example, that does not prove abuse or incompetence. It does tell you that the abstract promise of accountability still needs more evidence.

The next chapter tests authority in one of its most tangible forms: money.

# Chapter 5 — Follow the Money, Follow the Power

Mission organizations talk about money in spiritual language for understandable reasons. Money supports missionaries, churches, translation, hospitals, education, relief, training, and infrastructure. Support also carries relational meaning: churches and individuals give because they want to participate in the work.

But financial systems are not merely technical. They reveal authority.

Who controls money can often decide what projects begin, whose salary is paid, which ministries expand, and how much freedom local leaders possess. If you want to understand a mission organization, follow both the money and the decision rights attached to it.

## “Support Raising” Is Not One System

Candidates often ask, “How much support do I need to raise?” That question may hide a dozen others.

Two agencies may each quote a €6,000 monthly target for a married couple while including very different categories. One may include salary, insurance, retirement, children's education, ministry expenses, emergency reserves, and home-assignment costs. Another may cover mainly living expenses and an agency assessment.

The headline number tells you little. Ask what is inside it.

## Who Owns the Money?

When a donor gives “to your ministry,” who legally controls the funds? Usually the answer is not simply the missionary. Depending on the system, contributions may become assets of the organization designated for a worker or ministry purpose.

That can be entirely appropriate. Candidates still need to know the consequences:

- Can funds be used for another purpose?
- What happens to surplus?
- What happens if you leave?
- Can funds move to another agency?
- Who owns supporter contact information?
- Who communicates changes to donors?

Do not wait until resignation to learn these rules.

## Support Deficits Reveal the Risk Model

Ask what happens if support falls.

Possible systems include salary reductions, ministry-account deficits, temporary subsidy, home fundraising, delayed deployment, or reassignment. Each distributes financial risk differently.

A pooled denominational sender and an individually supported faith mission can both be legitimate. The candidate should know who carries volatility and what happens when the plan does not work.

## Fundraising May Follow You Overseas

For many individually supported missionaries, donor relationships continue throughout service through newsletters, calls, church visits, and support rebuilding during home assignment. Historical missionary stress research identified donor maintenance and workload among recurring stressors.[^6]

That does not make support raising unhealthy. It means fundraising and communication belong inside the real job description.

Ask a current worker how much attention maintaining financial support requires in a normal year.

## “Overhead” Is an Incomplete Measure

Candidates sometimes compare agencies by administrative percentage. Low overhead can look virtuous. Yet central spending may fund safeguarding, finance controls, counseling, cybersecurity, legal compliance, children's education support, training, and evacuation systems.

An organization that minimizes every central cost can also underinvest in the systems that keep people safe and ministry sustainable.

Do not ask only “What percentage goes to the field?” Ask: **What does central infrastructure actually provide?**

Audits or external financial accreditations can provide useful evidence about particular financial controls. They should not be treated as certificates of total organizational health.

## Money Reveals Cross-Cultural Power

The issue becomes especially important in local partnerships.

An organization may describe a ministry as locally led while expatriates retain control over major budgets, salaries, property, donor communication, and strategic funding. Local leadership may be real yet constrained.

This does not imply that all foreign funding should immediately be transferred without controls. Donor restrictions, legal duties, and fiduciary responsibilities may matter. The question is whether power is described honestly.

Ask: **Can local Christian leaders make a major strategic decision that foreign donors would dislike?** If the practical answer is no because funding would disappear, the foreign organization still holds significant power.

## Financial Differences Communicate

Foreign missionaries may receive salaries, insurance, education support, and mobility unavailable to local coworkers. Perfect equality may be impossible or inappropriate because tax systems, responsibilities, and family obligations differ.

Candidates should still ask how differences are understood, who sets local salaries, and whether disparities undermine proclaimed partnership.

Money communicates. Sometimes it communicates more loudly than organizational values.

## Build a Real Household Budget

A support target is not enough. Model actual life: housing, food, transport, tax, insurance, retirement, emergency savings, language study, travel, children's education, home assignment, ministry expenses, and currency fluctuation.

“God will provide” does not mean “I do not need to understand the budget.” Christian trust in God's provision is compatible with responsible planning.

Likewise, debt should neither be ignored nor automatically treated as spiritual unreadiness. Student loans, family obligations, and professional debt are financial realities that must fit the proposed system.

## Exit Reveals Financial Culture

Before joining, ask what happens to remaining funds, donor relationships, ministry equipment, benefits, and support records when a missionary leaves.

Understanding exit terms does not communicate disloyalty. It clarifies the agreement.

## Financial Pressure Can Distort Reporting

Missionary storytelling often supports fundraising. Workers naturally want to show fruit, urgency, and impact. When support is precarious, a subtle incentive can develop to sharpen stories or report only success.

The point is systemic, not accusatory. Ask whether the organization rewards truthful reporting when ministry is slow and statistics are unimpressive. A healthy financial system should not make exaggeration feel necessary for survival.

## Understand Benefits as Part of the System

Candidates sometimes compare agencies by monthly support target while ignoring benefits that may become decisive during a long career. Ask about health insurance, disability, emergency evacuation, retirement or pension arrangements, parental leave, home-assignment travel, education support, and what happens to these benefits during a support deficit or transition.

A higher support target may finance a stronger safety net. A lower target may be entirely responsible because the worker receives benefits through another system. The point is not to maximize benefits; it is to compare like with like.

For married candidates, ask whether both spouses are insured and how the organization understands each spouse's status. For children, ask what coverage exists in the destination country and during travel or home assignment. For professionals, ask whether professional liability is separate from ordinary health or travel insurance.

Do not assume the recruiter can answer every technical detail. A healthy response may be, “Our finance or benefits specialist should explain that.” The ability to route a question to the right person is itself evidence of organizational competence.

## Ask What Financial Failure Looks Like

Another revealing question is: **What has the organization done during a real financial shortfall?**

Did it reduce field budgets? Use reserves? Ask missionaries to raise more? Delay new appointments? Cut member-care capacity? Protect salaries while cutting local partnerships? Different choices communicate priorities.

One crisis does not define an organization forever. But the way leaders describe hard financial decisions can reveal whether the institution understands itself as stewarding people and ministry rather than merely protecting expansion.

Money reveals how an organization distributes risk, responsibility, and power. The next chapter asks what happens when someone with less power needs protection.

# Chapter 6 — When People Are Vulnerable

Every Christian organization can write a good sentence about caring for people. The real test begins when care becomes expensive.

A complaint may threaten a respected leader. A safeguarding report may damage reputation. A missionary may need long-term counseling. A child may say something nobody wants to hear. A worker may accuse a supervisor of serious misconduct.

These are moments when values stop being inexpensive.

## Start With Response, Not Reputation

Do not begin by asking whether an organization has ever had a serious problem. Every large enough human institution eventually faces serious problems.

Ask instead: **What happens when a serious problem is reported?**

A trustworthy organization does not need a history without failure. It needs a credible way of facing failure truthfully.

## Spiritualized Power Needs Particular Care

Jesus rejects leadership modeled on domination and teaches His disciples to understand greatness through service (Matthew 20:25–28). Peter tells elders not to domineer over people entrusted to them (1 Peter 5:1–4). These passages do not give us an HR manual, but they establish a Christian moral problem with domination.

Mission organizations possess an additional vulnerability because organizational authority can be spiritualized. A supervisor saying “This is the team's decision” is one thing. A supervisor implying “God's will for you is to obey my assignment” is making a much larger claim.

Spiritual language becomes dangerous when it makes ordinary organizational decisions immune from scrutiny.

## Use “Spiritual Abuse” Carefully

Research on spiritual abuse is still developing. Recurring themes include coercive control, manipulation, misuse of Scripture, intimidation, isolation, and demands for excessive submission.[^7]

Not every disagreement, correction, strict policy, poor decision, or difficult leader is spiritual abuse. Using the term carelessly weakens it and can make ordinary authority impossible. Yet caution about terminology must not become an excuse to minimize genuine coercion.

Describe behavior accurately. Ask what the leader did, what authority they claimed, whether the worker could appeal, whether Scripture was used to silence concern, and whether departure was framed as disobedience to God.

## Safeguarding Is a System

Documented historical mission-abuse investigations show why background checks alone are insufficient.[^8] A safeguarding system includes prevention, training, reporting, investigation, external reporting where required, survivor care, leadership accountability, and secure record handling.

No screening system can guarantee that a harmful person will never enter. A serious system makes harm harder to commit, easier to report, more likely to be investigated properly, and less likely to be hidden to protect ministry reputation.

## Children Need Their Own Protection

Parents may accept significant missionary risk for themselves. Children do not simply inherit unlimited adult consent to risk.

A child should never be taught, explicitly or implicitly, that reporting harm would damage the gospel or ruin the parents' ministry. Historical investigations of missionary boarding-school abuse demonstrate how devastating that logic can become.[^8]

Parents should ask who supervises children, what training and screening exists, what happens after a disclosure, whether children can access help outside immediate field leadership, and how legally required external reporting is handled.

## The Reporting Route Must Bypass the Problem

A grievance system fails if the only route is worker → supervisor. What if the supervisor is the concern?

Depending on the issue, bypass routes may include regional leadership, HR, safeguarding personnel, board channels, independent investigators, or civil authorities. Not every complaint requires external investigation, but serious allegations involving senior leadership or safeguarding can create obvious conflicts if the accused institution is the only investigator.

Ask for the process, not confidential personnel details. A responsible organization may be unable to tell you sensitive case facts while still explaining how the system works.

## Historical Failure Still Matters—Carefully

Should a decades-old scandal affect your decision today? Sometimes, but not simplistically.

Historical failure can reveal culture, governance weaknesses, and institutional response under pressure. It does not automatically define every current worker or team. The proper questions are: What happened? How strong is the evidence? How did leaders respond? What did later leadership do? What changed? Is reform visible in current systems?

Organizational repentance is more than an apology. It can include truthful acknowledgment, responsibility, care for harmed people, personnel consequences where warranted, system reform, independent review, and sustained change.

Forgiveness should never be used as a mechanism for preventing accountability.

## Member Care Is More Than Counseling

At its best, member care includes preparation, medical support, counseling, coaching, family care, crisis response, home assignment, and reentry. The existence of a department does not prove workers experience care.

Ask who provides care, whether providers are qualified for their role, whether workers can self-refer, whether independent counseling is allowed, who pays, and what is confidential.

Confidentiality has architecture. If an organization says counseling is confidential, ask who can access records, where they are stored, what exceptions apply, and whether a field leader or HR can see clinical detail.

## Can Bad News Travel Upward?

One of the most revealing questions in the book is whether bad news can travel upward.

Can a worker say: “Our strategy is failing.” “The numbers are misleading.” “I think my supervisor is harming the team.” “I am depressed.” “I made a serious mistake.” “I think a boundary was crossed.”

Research on psychological safety and employee voice finds that willingness to speak is influenced by whether people expect interpersonal or organizational consequences.[^9] Mission organizations are not ordinary corporations, but leaders still need truthful information.

Ask current or former workers for an actual example of serious disagreement. Healthy leadership can reject a complaint; the question is whether people remain heard and are treated fairly rather than retaliated against.

## Safety Is Not the Highest Christian Good

This chapter must not imply that a trustworthy organization removes risk. Mission can involve real danger, loss, and hardship.

The necessary distinction is between **costly obedience** and **preventable harm produced by negligence, coercion, or concealment**.

Learning a difficult language may be costly. Normalizing destructive chronic overwork is something else. Serving in a dangerous context may be faithful. Ignoring a known safeguarding threat is something else.

## Member Care Should Exist Before the Crisis

Care is easiest to notice when something has already gone badly. A stronger model treats care as part of normal missionary life.

Before deployment, that can mean realistic preparation, health assessment, marital or family conversations, and clarity about where help comes from. During field service it can include regular check-ins, coaching, medical support, peer community, and access to confidential professional care. During home assignment or permanent return, it may include debriefing and reentry support.

This does not mean every organization needs an internal clinical department. Many small organizations use external counselors, physicians, or specialist networks. Ask whether the pathway is clear and whether workers can use it without feeling that seeking help will automatically threaten their organizational standing.

The question becomes especially important when the caregiver and evaluator are the same person. A team leader may be an excellent pastoral presence, but some problems are difficult to disclose to the person who also evaluates performance. Independent care routes protect both the worker and the supervisor from role confusion.

## Safeguarding Discoverability Matters

A policy hidden on an internal drive that workers cannot find is weaker in practice than a clearly communicated system. Ask a current worker—not only a recruiter—what they would do if they needed to report suspected abuse or serious misconduct.

Can they identify the route without searching for twenty minutes? Can the route bypass a team leader? Is there an external option where appropriate? Are children and vulnerable adults explicitly covered? Does the organization distinguish pastoral confidentiality from legal reporting duties?

The answers do not need to be technically sophisticated. They need to be usable.

## Do Not Confuse a Strong Policy With Proven Implementation

A detailed policy is positive evidence about policy quality. It is not yet proof that the system works under pressure. That requires other evidence: worker knowledge, leadership behavior, historical response, external review, or documented reform.

The reverse is also important. An organization with a shorter public policy may still have strong internal practice. Limited website detail should trigger questions, not a guilty verdict.

A credible organization does not promise that nothing bad will happen. It shows that when people become vulnerable, reports have somewhere to go, leaders are not above review, children have protection, confidentiality is meaningful, and ministry reputation does not outrank truth.

# Chapter 7 — Can This Organization Actually Support the Work?

A good organization can still be the wrong organization.

Suppose an agency is doctrinally faithful, financially transparent, relationally healthy, and serious about protecting people. You want to become a Bible translator working in an unwritten language. The organization has almost no linguistic training, translation consulting, literacy expertise, or field experience in that work.

The problem is not trustworthiness. It is capability.

## Capability Follows the Role

Return to Chapter 1. What are you being sent to do? Now ask what the organization must be able to provide for that work to be carried out responsibly.

A church planter may need language and cultural learning, field mentorship, theological development, and long-term team support. A physician may need licensing assistance, professional liability coverage, medical infrastructure, and scheduling compatible with professional responsibility. A translation role may require linguistic training, consulting, technical systems, and community partnership. A restricted-field assignment may require security expertise and carefully governed communications.

Different responsibilities create different capability requirements.

## Training Volume Is Not Competence

An organization may say, “Every candidate completes twelve weeks of training.” That tells you duration, not capability.

Education and workplace-learning research distinguishes exposure, retained learning, demonstrated competence, and transfer into real work. Retrieval practice and spaced learning can improve durable knowledge; feedback quality matters; transfer depends partly on field and supervisor support.[^10]

The better question is: **What should a missionary be able to do after this training, and how do you know?**

Training should sometimes be able to say “not yet.” Otherwise it may function mainly as orientation. That does not justify arbitrary gatekeeping. Requirements should correspond to real responsibilities.

## Longer Is Not Automatically Better

Some agencies require substantial pre-field education. Others expect much development through field apprenticeship. Both can work.

Ask what is taught beforehand, what is learned on field, who supervises, how progress is assessed, and whether the field actually protects the learning that headquarters says matters.

Duration is an input. Competence is the goal.

## Language Policy Reveals Mission Philosophy

Some roles require modest language ability. Others require preaching, counseling, theological discussion, conflict resolution, or translation. “Fluent” is too vague.

A person may shop, joke, and travel comfortably while still being unable to teach Scripture or understand subtle relational dynamics. Language standards should follow ministry tasks.

Living in another country helps, but immersion is not an instructional system. Serious language development usually requires interaction, structured learning, repeated practice, feedback, and protected time.

Ask what happens when visible ministry competes with language learning. What the field rewards will eventually outrank what the training manual says.

## Field Transfer Matters

Many missionary competencies are open-ended: contextualization, leadership, conflict, discipleship, cultural interpretation. They must be adapted to changing situations.

A stronger pathway resembles:

> explanation → demonstration → practice → feedback → supervised real responsibility → increasing independence.

Ask who will develop you after arrival, how often they will observe your work, what correction looks like, and what happens if the mentoring relationship is poor.

Missionary development should be a lifecycle rather than a one-time event.

## Cultural Capability Includes Correctability

Cross-cultural competence is not merely knowledge *about* local people. Ask whether local Christians participate in training, whether field history is taught, whether workers are expected to gain real language competence, and whether local leaders can correct expatriate assumptions.

An organization with excellent technical infrastructure but little local correctability has a meaningful capability gap.

## Professional Roles Need Professional Systems

Missionary willingness does not replace professional competence. Medicine, counseling, aviation, teaching, and other regulated roles may require licensure, professional liability, and specialist standards.

A MedSend analysis of healthcare missionaries who left field service included reports of workers arriving where professional licensing or work authorization had not been adequately resolved, sometimes leaving them unable to perform the work they had prepared to do.[^11] These were worker reports rather than independently adjudicated legal findings, so the proper lesson is limited: professional feasibility should be verified before relocation.

Ask: **Who is responsible for confirming that I can legally and professionally perform this role?**

## Security and Technology Should Match the Field

Not every organization needs sophisticated security infrastructure. Organizations working in high-risk environments may hold sensitive information about missionaries, local Christians, seekers, and churches. Capability may therefore include account security, controlled access, secure communications, incident response, and field-specific threat assessment.

The principle is proportionality. A low-risk assignment does not need intelligence-agency procedures. A high-risk ministry should not improvise digital security worker by worker.

## Specialist Capacity Can Be Outsourced

International mission can involve visas, tax, employment status, insurance, sanctions, safeguarding law, and professional licensing. No recruiter should be expected to master every jurisdiction.

A smaller agency may responsibly use immigration lawyers, accountants, insurers, counselors, and external security advisers. The key question is whether the function exists and someone owns the process.

## The Minimum Viable Organization Principle

Direct church sending can be legitimate. Removing a conventional agency does not remove the functions a responsible sender normally performs. Someone still needs to handle finances, insurance, legal compliance, safeguarding, security, crisis response, pastoral care, and reentry where those functions are relevant.

The question is never simply “Do we have an agency?” It is: **Are the functions this missionary responsibility requires actually covered?**

A trustworthy organization should also know its limits. One of the strongest answers a recruiter can give is, “Another organization may be better equipped for that particular role.”

## Ask What New Workers Repeatedly Arrive Unprepared For

Training systems improve when the field can criticize them. Ask experienced field leaders: **What do new workers repeatedly arrive unable to do?**

If the same answer appears across several cohorts—weak language habits, poor conflict skills, unrealistic cultural expectations, thin theology, inadequate professional preparation—the organization should be able to show how the training system responds.

This creates a feedback loop: field experience identifies gaps, preparation changes, new workers arrive, and the field evaluates again. An organization that has trained missionaries for fifty years may have valuable tradition. A stronger sign of organizational learning is the ability to name what it has changed because the old approach was insufficient.

## Distinguish Credential From Capability

A degree or certificate can be meaningful evidence of preparation. It should not become a substitute for competence. Likewise, an experienced pastor or professional should not assume prior expertise makes mission-specific preparation unnecessary.

Ask what each prerequisite is supposed to produce or verify. If the requirement costs substantial time or money, the organization should be able to explain the competency it serves. Where appropriate, it may be reasonable for experienced candidates to demonstrate competence through alternate pathways.

The goal is neither anti-education minimalism nor institutional credentialism. It is role-appropriate readiness.

## Development Should Continue After Competence

Passing a language benchmark or completing orientation should not mark the end of development. Missionaries may later become team leaders, trainers, supervisors, or specialists. A healthy organization should have some way to keep developing workers as responsibilities change.

Ask long-term missionaries what they have learned through the organization after their first term. If all formal development ends at deployment, the agency may be treating preparation as a gate rather than a lifecycle.
