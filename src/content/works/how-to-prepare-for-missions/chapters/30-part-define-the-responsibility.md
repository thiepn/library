Part II established that uncertainty does not make preparation useless.

Character travels. Real ministry practice matters where ministry responsibility is likely. A durable vocation can remain valuable even when plans change. A deliberate learning system helps training become usable competence rather than accumulated exposure.

But broad preparation has a limit.

Eventually you have to stop asking, "How should I prepare for missions?"

The category is too large.

A church planter, physician, Bible translator, teacher, administrator, engineer, theological educator, business owner, member-care worker, and logistics specialist may all serve within cross-cultural mission. Their responsibilities differ too much for one preparation plan to fit all of them.

Part III therefore narrows the question.

First: What are you actually preparing to do?

Then: Which capabilities must reside in you, and which belong elsewhere in the body and system around you?

Finally: Where would that responsibility actually fit among real people, churches, languages, needs, and existing Christian work?

Only after those questions become clearer does highly specialized preparation become wise.
