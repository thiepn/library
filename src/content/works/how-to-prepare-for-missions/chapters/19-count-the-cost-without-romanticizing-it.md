Missionary preparation eventually becomes very practical.

Money. Debt. Insurance. Medication. Visas. Housing. Emergency travel. Children's schooling. Security. Retirement. Flights. Legal restrictions.

Theology still governs the discussion.

It does not make logistics disappear.

## The Cost Belongs in the Decision

Luke 14 uses the image of calculating whether a tower can be completed inside a much larger call to costly discipleship.

It does not provide a missionary budget template.

It does establish that Christian commitment and sober reckoning are not enemies.

Responsible preparation asks what the assignment actually requires.

## Money

Estimate startup costs, normal living expenses, taxes, insurance, travel, language study, professional fees, education, emergencies, retirement, and re-entry where relevant.

Different funding models can be legitimate: donor support, salary, business income, bivocational work, organizational funding, or combinations.

Do not spiritualize one model.

Funding strategy must fit law, role, ethics, organization, and household reality.

## Debt

Debt is not automatically disqualifying.

Its significance depends on amount, payment burden, interest, income, dependents, support model, and legal obligations.

The question is whether the financial structure makes the assignment irresponsible or unsustainable.

## Health

Prepare honestly.

Consider current conditions, medication access, specialist care, insurance, emergency treatment, disability accommodation, and mental-health support.

A health condition does not make a Christian less faithful.

It may alter which assignments are feasible.

That is role fit, not spiritual ranking.

## Law and Immigration

Missionaries must take law seriously unless obedience to God creates genuine conflict.

Immigration, employment, professional, reporting, and ministry regulations vary by jurisdiction and can change.

Do not rely on generalized missionary folklore.

Use current official or qualified legal/professional sources for the actual assignment.

## Security

Some assignments involve genuine danger.

Christian mission does not promise safety.

Neither does it sanctify unnecessary exposure.

A good security process asks what the threat is, how likely it is, who bears the risk, what can reduce it, what mission benefit depends on accepting it, who decides, what happens during crisis, and whether foreign security decisions transfer risk onto locally rooted Christians.

Security is not merely self-protection.

It has relational consequences.

## Faith Is Not Recklessness

One dangerous missionary rhetoric equates caution with fear.

A second makes safety the supreme good.

Both distort Christian judgment.

There are assignments where Christians knowingly accept serious danger. There are also dangers created by negligence, poor planning, untrained leadership, inadequate safeguarding, reckless travel, or ignored medical advice.

Suffering for Christ and suffering because of preventable incompetence are not the same thing.

Christians may voluntarily accept costly responsibilities for Christ. No mission leader or organization may use that theology to manufacture avoidable danger or silence questions about preventable harm.

Responsible preparation also does not require every missionary to possess the safety infrastructure available to a wealthy expatriate. Insurance, evacuation options, specialist medicine, reserves, and legal support differ radically across countries and sending systems.

The standard is not one expensive package. It is honest understanding of risks, obligations, available safeguards, and the people who will bear the consequences.

## Build Contingencies

Before departure, know what happens if support falls, health deteriorates, a visa fails, political conditions change, the family needs to leave, the organization fails, or you become unable to do the intended role.

Contingency planning is not an admission that God is absent.

It is stewardship under uncertainty.

## Part IV Reflection

1. Which communication functions does your actual role require, and which language goals are you pursuing mainly because "missionaries should be fluent"?
2. Who can correct your cultural interpretations and ministry decisions in the context you are considering, and do those people possess meaningful authority or merely advisory access?
3. Discuss with your household, church, or team the unanswered question about the assignment that could carry the greatest consequence if ignored.
4. Which cost, risk, or organizational concern deserves one concrete next step now rather than another month of vague worry?
