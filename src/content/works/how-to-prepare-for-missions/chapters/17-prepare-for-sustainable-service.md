Missionary endurance is often discussed as though it belongs mainly inside the missionary.

Develop resilience. Build grit. Manage stress. Stay spiritually strong.

Those things can matter.

But sustainability is not purely an individual trait.

A healthy person can deteriorate inside an unhealthy system. A struggling person may function well with appropriate support, boundaries, care, and role fit.

Preparation therefore needs a broader question:

**Can this person, household, role, team, organization, and context sustain faithful service together?**

## Resilience Is Not Unlimited Endurance

A missionary may experience legitimate hardship.

Language learning is tiring. Cross-cultural adjustment is disorienting. Conflict hurts. Ministry disappoints. Bereavement, illness, persecution, political instability, and loneliness may become real.

Christian endurance matters.

Second Corinthians 4 reminds us that Christian ministry is carried by frail people and may involve real suffering. It does not provide a burnout-prevention model or an organizational member-care policy.

The distinction matters because "be resilient" can become a way organizations avoid examining excessive workload, poor supervision, abusive leadership, chronic understaffing, unresolved conflict, or lack of rest.

Resilience helps a Christian face unavoidable hardship faithfully.

It should never become an organizational demand that workers tolerate indefinitely what responsible leadership could correct.

## Know Your Patterns

Self-awareness helps.

What happens when you become tired? Do you isolate? Become irritable? Overwork? Avoid conflict? Become controlling? What early signs show that you are deteriorating? Who can tell when you are not doing well?

Psychological assessments may help candidates and organizations notice patterns.

They should remain bounded tools rather than spiritual verdicts.

There is no validated ideal missionary personality.

## Build Help Before Crisis

Do not wait for collapse to decide who can help.

Before departure, identify pastoral relationships, friends outside the reporting chain, professional counseling options, medical care, emergency contacts, member-care structures, and escalation paths.

A worker should know whether appropriate care can be accessed without making every private struggle an employment matter.

The detailed confidentiality and conflict-of-interest questions belong to Chapter 18, because they are organizational design questions.

## Rest Is Ordinary Creaturely Life

Christian service involves work.

It also involves creaturely limits.

Sleep, rest, exercise, friendship, time with family, medical care, worship, and ordinary recreation are not techniques that guarantee longevity.

They are ordinary parts of embodied life.

Some seasons will disrupt routines.

The problem comes when exceptional demands become permanent expectations.

## Attrition Is Multicausal

Historical mission research, including the ReMAP II work published in *Worth Keeping* in 2007, examined missionary retention and attrition across multiple contexts.

That research is useful historically. It is not a 2026 global missionary attrition census.

The more durable lesson is that departure can involve interacting factors such as role mismatch, conflict, family strain, health, organizational problems, finances, unmet expectations, or lack of support.

No one causal ranking should be universalized.

Sometimes leaving is itself the faithful decision.

Sustainability means building conditions where avoidable breakdown is less likely and honest help is possible.

The next chapter turns from the worker's support needs to the organization that shapes many of those conditions.
