A missionary may arrive determined to be humble and still become the center.

Why?

Because power does not exist only in personality.

It can come from money, passports, education, donor relationships, organizational authority, control of salaries, access to international institutions, language, technology, family networks, class, denominational status, or government relationships.

A kind foreign worker can still occupy a structurally dominant position.

Cross-cultural preparation therefore has to ask not only how to relate respectfully but **who actually decides**.

## Authority Is More Than Consultation

Organizations often say they value local input.

That can mean very different things.

A leader may be informed, consulted, asked for advice, invited to meetings, able to recommend, able to decide, able to veto, or able to control resources.

Those are not equivalent.

Meaningful local Christian agency requires more than the ability to offer opinions after outsiders retain all consequential authority.

This does not mean the foreign worker should have no authority.

Responsibility sometimes justifies defined authority. A physician may hold clinical authority because of competence. A project manager may control parts of a project. A donor may rightly require transparent financial accountability.

The question is whether authority corresponds to real responsibility or expands because one party possesses more resources and mobility.

## Expertise Is Real but Bounded

Suppose an expatriate theologian genuinely knows more about a specialized subject than a young local seminary faculty.

That competence matters.

It may justify significant teaching responsibility.

It does not automatically justify controlling the institution, choosing all curricula, managing funding indefinitely, selecting future leaders, or overriding local governance outside the specialist responsibility.

Likewise, local rootedness does not create infallibility.

Locally rooted leaders can misuse power, act incompetently, tolerate abuse, mismanage money, or teach error.

Foreign and local Christians alike remain under Scripture, answerable to Christ, and correctable within the wider body.

The alternative to foreign domination is not romanticized local authority.

It is accountable interdependence.

## Money Changes Relationships

Money can accelerate ministry.

It can also distort decision-making.

The person who controls funding may gain influence even when nobody formally grants it.

A missionary can unintentionally train a community to ask what the donor will approve rather than what the church should do.

Support may be entirely appropriate. The question is what the support produces.

Who determines priorities? Can leaders disagree without risking money? Do imported resources create dependencies that nobody planned? Does a funding arrangement move decision authority toward people farthest from the consequences?

These are questions about power, not arguments against generosity.

Detailed financial feasibility belongs to Chapter 19. Here, money is one example of how resources can shift authority.

## Strengthen Responsibility Beyond One Outsider

Where a ministry's purpose includes locally rooted ownership, leadership, or reproducibility, healthy service should normally strengthen capacity beyond dependence on one foreign worker.

That may happen through local leadership development, shared decision-making, institutional strengthening, professional training, handover, or multiplication.

This is not a rule that every outsider must leave rapidly. Some specialist or collaborative roles may remain genuinely useful for decades.

The key question is whether the ministry's structure fits its purpose and whether one person's indispensability has become a substitute for a healthy system.

## Map Actual Power

Do not presume all foreigners dominate all local actors, or all local actors lack power.

Ask concretely:

- Who sets strategy?
- Who controls resources?
- Who hires and removes people?
- Who can stop a project?
- Whose expertise is trusted automatically?
- Who can criticize the person with the most power without retaliation?
- Who bears the consequences of a bad decision?

Power is not made righteous by nationality.

It should be made visible enough to become accountable.

Team life is where many of these dynamics become ordinary rather than theoretical.
