Culture-training courses often begin by giving the learner categories: individualist, collectivist, high-context, low-context, high power distance, honor-shame, direct, indirect.

These frameworks can help.

They can also become a sophisticated form of stereotyping.

A missionary learns a national profile and begins seeing people through the profile rather than allowing people to contradict it.

Cross-cultural preparation therefore needs a different goal.

Not mastery.

**Learnability.**

## Culture Is More Than National Personality

People belong to overlapping worlds.

A person may be shaped by family, region, ethnicity, language, generation, education, profession, church, migration, class, political history, online communities, and many other influences.

National culture models aggregate patterns.

They do not tell you exactly who the individual in front of you is.

Use them as questions, not answers.

Instead of saying, "People here avoid direct conflict," ask, "How is disagreement normally communicated in this relationship, institution, generation, and setting?"

That shift matters.

## Peter Needed Correction Too

Acts 10-11 is not an anthropology method. The narrative concerns God's initiative, Gentile inclusion, purity boundaries, the Spirit, and Peter's theological correction.

Yet the episode destroys one comfortable assumption: God's messenger is not necessarily the person whose categories require the least correction.

That theological lesson supports humility. The practical culture-learning method that follows is prudential and informed by cross-cultural experience rather than extracted from Acts.

## Observation Before Interpretation

Early field life generates constant interpretation.

Why did nobody answer directly? Why was the meeting late? Why did the pastor change the plan? Why did everyone agree publicly and object later? Why did a family make a decision together? Why did someone refuse help that seemed obviously beneficial?

The outsider naturally explains.

Often too quickly.

A useful discipline is to separate what happened from what you think it meant.

If a meeting begins forty minutes after the stated time, the observation is simple. "People here do not value punctuality" is an interpretation.

The reason may involve transportation, hierarchy, expectations about the stated time, relationship priorities, one delayed leader, or this particular group.

Cross-cultural humility is partly the discipline of delaying explanation.

## Participation Teaches What Profiles Cannot

Culture is learned inside relationships.

Attend. Listen. Eat with people. Join ordinary church life. Watch how decisions occur. Notice who speaks and who does not. Learn what people laugh at. Observe how elders are treated, how hospitality works, what changes between formal and informal settings, and how people describe their own society.

A country briefing may prepare questions.

Participation provides provisional answers.

Even then, locally rooted people must be able to correct the outsider's interpretations.

Someone must be able to say, "That sounded arrogant," "Nobody understood what you meant," "You are reading this conflict incorrectly," or "That example does not work here."

Cultural humility depends partly on ordinary humility.

The field does not create teachability. It exposes whether teachability is present.

## Contextualization Has Boundaries

Cultural learning does not mean every local practice is right.

Every culture, including the missionary's own, contains wisdom, beauty, common grace, idolatry, injustice, and assumptions requiring biblical correction.

The gospel is not identical with Western culture.

Neither is it identical with any receiving culture.

Contextualization therefore requires two forms of humility: the outsider must stop assuming personal culture is Christianity, and every culture remains under Scripture.

The exact judgments can be difficult and sometimes disputed.

That uncertainty is not solved by cultural arrogance in either direction.

Appendix C provides a simple observation-and-revision record. Its purpose is not to make you the expert on someone else's people. It is to help you become increasingly capable of living, listening, serving, and being corrected there.

That leads to a harder question.

Even culturally sensitive outsiders may still hold most of the power.
