A poorly designed missionary competency list can make a candidate feel that faithfulness requires becoming six people.

Learn theology. Learn language. Become excellent at evangelism. Become a skilled teacher. Understand culture. Handle conflict. Manage money. Know safeguarding. Understand security. Become emotionally resilient. Lead teams. Develop technical competence. Communicate with donors. Support your family. Learn administration.

The list contains many real responsibilities.

The error appears when we assume every responsibility must become an individual competency.

That is not how a body works.

## Ask Where the Capability Must Live

First Corinthians 12 gives the church an image of differentiated interdependence.

Paul's body metaphor establishes differentiated gifting and interdependence within the church. It does not provide a modern mission-organization competency chart. The ownership approach used here is a prudential extension of that theological principle combined with the ordinary reality that complex work distributes expertise among people and institutions.

Instead of asking, "Do I have every capability this assignment could need?" ask:

**What capability must exist, and where should it responsibly reside?**

[[FIG04]]

Possible locations include the missionary, household, teammate, local church, sending church, mission organization, locally rooted Christian leader, professional specialist, or external institution.

The important question is not whether the worker personally owns every capability.

It is whether the responsibility is actually covered.

## Personal Responsibility Still Matters

Distributed competence can be misused.

A worker who lacks competence may say, "Nobody has to know everything. The team has that covered."

Sometimes that is exactly right.

Sometimes it is an excuse.

The distinction depends on role ownership.

If you are the person teaching a class, sufficient teaching and content competence belong to you. If you perform surgery, clinical competence belongs to you. If you control a project budget, you need enough financial competence to carry that responsibility faithfully even if an accountant supports the organization. If you work directly with children, safeguarding knowledge cannot remain entirely in someone else's office.

If the task belongs to you, you need enough competence to perform it responsibly and to recognize when specialist help is required.

Competence includes knowing your limits.

## Capability Across the Body and System

Some capabilities belong directly to the individual: professional competence, language functions, Bible teaching, administration, interpersonal communication, or daily safety practices.

Some belong partly to a household. A family assignment cannot be assessed as though only one person existed.

Some belong to a team: leadership, specialist knowledge, administration, training, logistics, pastoral support, or technical work.

Churches and mission organizations may supply theological accountability, supervision, financial systems, safeguarding processes, member care, conflict systems, training, and crisis support.

Locally rooted pastors, theologians, professionals, ministry leaders, believers, institutions, and churches may already possess the expertise outsiders assume needs importing. Their competence may exceed the missionary's. Their contextual judgment may be indispensable. Their authority may properly govern work the outsider joins.

Some responsibilities should remain with qualified specialists: complex medical care, legal advice, professional psychological treatment, formal security assessment, specialized engineering, regulated financial work, or professional safeguarding investigation.

Christian community does not eliminate expertise.

## Mission Systems Need Capability Coverage

Consider a hypothetical assignment.

A small team plans to support an existing church network through theological education and youth discipleship.

Potential capabilities include theological teaching, local-language communication, youth safeguarding, curriculum development, administration, conflict handling, finance, cultural interpretation, pastoral care, and digital security.

It would be absurd to require every member to become independently expert in every domain.

The theology instructor may need substantial theological and teaching competence. Every adult working with youth needs appropriate safeguarding knowledge, while the organization needs stronger reporting and investigation systems. A financial administrator may carry technical bookkeeping responsibility. Local church leaders may hold primary contextual and ministry authority. A qualified counselor may be available externally rather than as a permanent team member.

The result is not lower readiness.

It is distributed readiness.

## The Super-Missionary Is a Bad System Design

Sometimes what looks like an exceptional missionary is actually a fragile system depending too heavily on one person.

One worker speaks the language best, controls donor relationships, teaches theology, manages finances, leads the team, handles conflict, knows officials, supervises local staff, and owns institutional knowledge.

Such a person may be highly capable.

The system can still be fragile.

If everything important requires one person, correction becomes harder, succession becomes harder, local authority remains limited, and departure can destabilize the work.

Competence should sometimes lead to greater distribution rather than greater centralization.

The point is not to make every organization flat or leaderless. It is to resist confusing indispensability with faithfulness.

## Interdependence Is Not Remedial Christianity

Some candidates feel ashamed when they need significant support.

They imagine the mature missionary as someone who can function anywhere, solve problems independently, adapt quickly, and need very little from others.

That image is neither biblically necessary nor operationally wise.

Interdependence is ordinary Christianity.

Paul depended on coworkers. Churches supported one another. Different believers supplied different gifts and resources.

Needing other people is not automatically evidence that preparation failed.

The useful question is whether dependence is healthy and visible: who supplies what, whether they are genuinely capable, what happens if they leave, and whether expectations are clear.

## Basic Literacy Still Matters

Even when another person owns specialist responsibility, you may need basic literacy.

You may not be the accountant, but you should understand enough financial responsibility to notice obvious problems in funds you control. You may not be the safeguarding lead, but you should know what to do if someone discloses abuse. You may not be a therapist, but you should recognize when a situation exceeds pastoral friendship and requires professional help.

## Who Judges Competence?

The person evaluating a competence should normally understand the competence.

A pastor may know your character well. That does not automatically qualify the pastor to determine whether your engineering work meets professional standards. A mission organization may understand cross-cultural placement. That does not automatically qualify it to certify clinical competence. A professional board may evaluate technical practice. That does not determine spiritual maturity.

No single evaluator should pretend to possess universal judgment.

Different forms of competence require different witnesses.

This prepares the next narrowing step.

Even a well-defined role with appropriate capability distribution still exists in abstraction until connected to a place, people, language, church, and actual gap.
