Christians who take Scripture seriously often want a verse for major decisions.

That instinct is understandable. If a preparation practice will require thousands of hours, significant money, or years of your life, you want to know whether God requires it.

Should every missionary attend seminary? Should every missionary learn the local language to advanced proficiency? Should candidates undergo psychological assessment? Must missionaries raise their own financial support? How long should pre-field training last?

Some Christians answer these questions with great confidence. Sometimes that confidence is warranted. Sometimes it exceeds what Scripture actually says.

A book about preparing for missions must therefore establish an authority hierarchy before giving extensive practical advice. Otherwise helpful recommendations can slowly acquire the force of divine law.

## Begin With What Christ Actually Commands

Matthew 28:18-20 gives the church a clear missionary responsibility. The risen Christ possesses all authority. His disciples are to make disciples among the nations, baptizing and teaching them to obey what he has commanded.

Whatever debates surround missionary strategy, some things are not merely preferences. The gospel matters. Disciple-making matters. Teaching obedience to Christ matters. Christian faithfulness matters.

The wider New Testament gives direct moral instruction relevant to anyone who serves. Christians must love, tell the truth, reject greed, flee sexual immorality, serve rather than dominate, pursue holiness, care for one another, and not distort the gospel.

Missionary strategy remains under ordinary Christian ethics. If a technique appears effective but requires deception, exploitation, manipulation, sexual misconduct, financial dishonesty, abusive authority, or doctrinal compromise, effectiveness cannot make it faithful.

## Scripture Often Commands Ends Without Specifying Every Modern Means

Take language learning.

Jesus commands disciple-making and teaching. Those responsibilities require intelligible communication. If you accept a role in which you must teach people directly in another language, serious language competence may become necessary.

But Matthew 28 does not provide a language proficiency scale. It does not prescribe a number of classroom hours or specify whether study should happen mainly before or after arrival.

The practical requirement emerges from the responsibility.

The same pattern appears elsewhere. Scripture requires financial integrity but does not provide a modern nonprofit accounting system. Scripture requires care for vulnerable people but does not provide a complete contemporary safeguarding policy. Scripture requires truthful teaching but does not determine which theological degree every missionary should earn.

A modern practice can therefore be wise - even practically necessary - without becoming a direct biblical command.

## Four Working Categories

For practical use, this book will often distinguish four kinds of statement. They are working categories rather than an exhaustive theology of authority.

### Biblical command

Some obligations carry direct Christian authority: love one another, tell the truth, make disciples, pursue holiness, reject sexual immorality, and care responsibly for those entrusted to you.

### Direct implication

Some conclusions are not stated in the exact modern form we use but follow strongly from biblical responsibility. If Scripture requires truthful teaching, teachers must understand the content they teach adequately. If a Christian accepts responsibility for people who cannot understand his language, communication competence becomes morally relevant.

### Prudential wisdom

Many preparation decisions belong here: timing language study, choosing a theological training path, setting financial reserves, designing assessment, or deciding how long mentorship should last. Evidence, experience, professional standards, church counsel, and context may make some answers much wiser than others.

### Optional practice

Some methods may help one person and be unnecessary for another: a conference, a particular journaling method, a personality inventory, a short exposure trip, or one study tool.

Optional does not mean useless. It means you should not bind another Christian's conscience where Scripture has not done so.

## Why the Distinction Matters

Without this authority filter, missionary preparation easily becomes legalistic.

One organization requires seminary, so seminary becomes "biblical preparation." Another expects two years of language school, so that duration becomes the faithful standard. A respected missionary found a short exposure visit helpful, so every prospective missionary is told to take one. A leader received a dramatic sense of calling, so candidates who have not experienced the same thing begin doubting whether God could use them.

The problem is not necessarily the method. It is the inflation of authority.

A recommendation moves silently from "This has helped us" to "This is wise" to "Serious missionaries should do this" and finally to "This is the biblical way."

Those sentences are not equivalent.

Later chapters will propose ways to classify readiness, define responsibilities, map competence, evaluate organizations, and decide what should be learned before or after arrival. Those frameworks are tools. They are not hidden verses of Scripture.

## When Wisdom Becomes Role-Critical

Biblical authority does not require pretending the Bible is a procedural manual for questions it does not directly address.

A modern requirement may nevertheless become practically mandatory because of law, ethics, competence, or the actual responsibility.

If you are preparing to work as a physician, Scripture does not command the licensing process of the country where you intend to practice. But if law and ordinary medical ethics require recognized professional competence, you cannot dismiss those requirements as merely human because the Bible does not mention them.

If you are preparing to teach Scripture publicly in a language you barely understand, Scripture gives no standardized language score. Yet if your limitation predictably prevents you from understanding people or communicating responsibly, developing language competence may be necessary for the assignment.

A modern requirement may therefore become role-critical without becoming universally biblical.

## The Danger in the Other Direction

If one error is turning wisdom into law, another is using the absence of a direct command to evade responsibility.

A prospective missionary might say, "The Bible never says I need formal preparation."

True.

It also never says you may accept responsibilities carelessly.

If your ministry involves counseling vulnerable people, professional boundaries matter. If you manage substantial funds, financial competence matters. If you teach doctrine, theological competence matters. If your decisions affect people's health or legal status, specialized knowledge may matter greatly.

The question is not merely, "Where is the verse commanding this training?"

Ask instead, "What responsibility have I accepted, and what does faithful stewardship of that responsibility require?"

Calling is where this distinction between revelation and wisdom becomes especially important.
